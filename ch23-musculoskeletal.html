Before the Test Cheat Sheet

One line each. ★ = tied to a study outcome, a quiz or a workbook item. Bold = most likely tested.

B1. The 20 biggest takeaways

  1. ★ Joint types: fibrous = does not move (skull sutures); cartilaginous = moves a little (vertebrae); synovial = moves freely, lined with a fluid-making membrane. (LO1, see Workbook SG1)
  2. ★ Signs of an inflamed joint: swelling, heat (warmth), redness, and pain or tenderness. Warmth plus tenderness = inflammation. (LO3, see Workbook SG2)
  3. ★ Dislocation = bones lose all contact. Subluxation = bones touch but are out of line. Contracture = shortened muscle limits motion. Ankylosis = joint is stiff or fixed. (LO6, see Workbook SG3)
  4. ★ Active vs passive ROM: active = the person moves it; passive = you move it while their muscles relax. Normally both are the same. (LO3, see Workbook SG4)
  5. ★ Limited ROM is the most sensitive sign of joint disease. Disease inside the joint limits all directions, active and passive. Injury outside the joint (one tendon or ligament) limits only some directions, mostly active. (LO3)
  6. ★ Exam rules: compare both sides; go head to toe, near to far, midline out; support each joint; move inflamed joints gently. (LO3)
  7. ★ RA vs OA: RA = both sides the same (symmetric), boggy MCPs, worse in the morning, movement helps. OA = one-sided pattern, hard bony nodes, worse later in the day, movement hurts. (LO6, see AP Q#21 and Q#22)
  8. ★ Heberden nodes = DIP (tip joint). Bouchard nodes = PIP (middle joint). Both are hard, nontender, and mean OA. Memory: "H is higher up the finger." (LO6, see Workbook SG9)
  9. ★ Swan-neck = PIP bends back too far plus DIP bends down. Boutonniere = knuckle pokes up as if through a buttonhole. Both = chronic RA. (LO6, see Workbook SG8)
  10. ★ Carpal tunnel tests: Phalen = backs of hands together, wrists bent 90°, hold 60 seconds. Tinel = tap over the median nerve at the wrist. Positive = numbness, burning, tingling. (LO4)
  11. ★ Knee fluid: bulge sign finds a small amount (4 to 8 mL). Ballottement finds a large amount. McMurray click = torn meniscus. (LO4)
  12. ★ Ottawa knee rules (x-ray if any): pain only at patella or fibula head; age 55 or older; cannot bend knee to 90°; cannot take 4 steps. (LO4)
  13. ★ Straight leg raise (Lasègue): raise straight leg, then bend foot up. Pain shooting down the leg = sciatica / herniated disc. (LO4)
  14. ★ True leg length: ASIS (front hip bone point) to medial malleolus (inner ankle bone). Equal or within 1 cm is normal. (LO4, see Workbook SG5)
  15. ★ Ortolani: infant on back, knees bent; bring legs together, then lift and spread out. A clunk = hip slipping back in = positive, refer. Do it at every visit until age 1. (LO4, LO5, see Workbook SG6)
  16. ★ Scoliosis forward bend: stand behind; check shoulders, scapulae, iliac crests; have them bend to toes; recheck shoulders, ribs, iliac crests. Rib hump = structural scoliosis. Order = 3, 2, 5, 7. (LO4, see AP Q#23)
  17. ★ Older adult (expected): kyphosis, slight bend at hips and knees, 3 to 5 cm height loss. Not expected: boggy MCPs, lordosis, osteoarthritis. (LO5, see AP Q#19)
  18. ★ Lordosis is normal in toddlers and in pregnancy. Pregnancy also adds neck flexion and a waddling gait. Flexible varus/valgus feet are normal in infants through school age. (LO5, see AP Q#20)
  19. ★ Get Up and Go test: rise from armchair, walk 10 feet, turn, walk back, sit. 12 seconds or more = higher fall risk. (LO4, LO7, see Workbook SG7)
  20. ★ Osteoporosis: DXA bone scan for all women by 65; FRAX gives 10-year fracture chance; bisphosphonates are first-line; hormone therapy is not recommended. (LO7, see AP FRAX case)

B5. Key landmarks and sequences

  • ★ Vertebrae = 33: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 3 or 4 coccyx. Memory: "breakfast at 7, lunch at 12, dinner at 5." (LO1)
  • ★ Spine landmarks: C7 and T1 stick out at the base of the neck; bottom tip of the scapula = T7–T8; line across the iliac crests = L4; dimples over the PSIS = S2. (LO1, see Fig 23-4)
  • ★ Rotator cuff = SITS: Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis. (LO1, see Fig 23-8)
  • ★ Shoulder dislocation: 95% are anterior (forward). (LO6)
  • ★ Order for each joint: inspect → palpate → active ROM → passive ROM if limited → muscle strength → special tests. (LO3)
  • ★ Bulge sign steps: stroke up the inner (medial) knee 2 to 3 times → tap the outer (lateral) side → watch the inner hollow for a bulge. (LO4, see Fig 23-32)
  • ★ Thomas test: pull one knee to chest; if the other thigh lifts off the table = hip flexion deformity. (LO4)
  • ★ Weight bearing line of the foot: heel → midfoot → between the 2nd and 3rd toes. (LO3)
  • ★ History clues: RA pain is worst on waking; OA pain is worse later in the day; tendinitis is worse in the morning and eases during the day. A tick bite raises Lyme disease risk. Joint pain 10 to 14 days after an untreated strep throat points to rheumatic fever. (LO2)

E1. The big picture

  • The musculoskeletal system = the body's bones, joints, and muscles (see Fig 23-1).
  • It has five jobs: support (lets us stand up straight), movement, protection of organs (brain, spinal cord, heart), making blood cells in the bone marrow (called hematopoiesis, "blood making"), and storage of minerals like calcium and phosphorus.
  • The skeleton has 206 bones. Over half of them are in the hands and feet.
  • Bone and cartilage are both special kinds of connective tissue (tissue that holds the body together).
  • A joint (also called an articulation) is where two or more bones meet. Joints are the "functional units" of the system. They give us the motion we need for activities of daily living (ADLs), like dressing and eating.
  • Muscles make up 40% to 50% of body weight.

F1. Bone remodeling, step by step

  1. Bone is alive. Its cells are always turning over and remodeling.
  2. Two jobs happen in a cycle: resorption (breaking old bone down) and deposition (laying new bone down). This keeps the skeleton fixed where it needs repair or replacement.
  3. Normal pattern: resorption = replacement. The bank account stays even.
  4. Osteoblasts (bone-building cells) make new bone. They have estrogen receptors, so estrogen helps them work.
  5. Ⓜ Merck Osteoclasts are the cells that resorb bone. Osteoblasts make the bone matrix and then mineralize it. Both are controlled by parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin, estrogen, vitamin D, cytokines (immune signal proteins, such as RANKL, which speeds up osteoclasts), and other local factors.
  6. Calcium storage: about 99% of the body's calcium is in bones and teeth. We lose some every day through skin, nails, hair, sweat, urine, and stool.
  7. If you eat too little calcium, the body releases extra parathyroid hormone. That causes bone resorption (calcium is pulled out of bone).
  8. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. Skin makes vitamin D from sunlight (UV light).
  9. Peak bone mass (most bone you will ever have) is reached in the mid-20s. Males peak later than females at all sites. The femoral neck (hip) peaks earlier than other sites. Merck says: peak bone mass is about age 30.
  10. After the peak, if resorption goes faster than replacement, bone density slowly drops. This is osteoporosis (porous bone with higher fracture risk).
  11. Women lose more bone than men with age. Lower estrogen in both sexes is part of the cause. Ⓜ Merck Women lose about 2% of bone per year for about 10 years after menopause.
  12. Use builds bone. Exercise (strength training, balance training, fast walking) adds bone mass. A sedentary life speeds bone loss.

Analogy: bone is a savings account. Childhood and teen years are the deposit years. Peak bone mass is the biggest balance you will ever have. After that, every year is a small withdrawal unless you keep depositing (exercise, calcium, vitamin D).

F2. How a synovial joint moves

  1. A muscle contracts and pulls on its tendon.
  2. The tendon pulls the bone, and the bone moves at the joint.
  3. Synovial fluid lets the cartilage-covered bone ends slide, like grease on gears.
  4. Movement also circulates synovial fluid. This is how avascular cartilage gets fed. (Joints need motion to stay healthy: "motion is lotion.")
  5. Ligaments stop motion in the wrong direction.
  6. Bursae let muscles and tendons glide over bone with little friction.
  7. In the spine, discs squeeze on one side and expand on the other as you bend. This elastic give lets the spine move and absorb shock.
  8. In the knee, the menisci cushion the femur and tibia.

F4. Muscle contraction basics (what the file covers)

  • There are three muscle types: skeletal, smooth, cardiac. This chapter covers skeletal (voluntary) muscle only.
  • A skeletal muscle = bundles of fibers (fasciculi). It attaches to bone by a tendon. When it contracts, the bone moves.
  • Each joint has prime movers (the main muscles that do a motion). The exam tests the prime-mover groups.
  • Muscle status and joint status depend on each other. Read them together.
  • Strength is graded 0 to 5 (see Section K and T).
  • Muscles grow with use and atrophy (shrink) with disuse, all through life.
  • Muscle growth is fastest in the teen growth spurt. It responds to growth hormone, adrenal androgens, and in boys testosterone.
  • Size and strength differ from person to person because of genetics, nutrition, and exercise.
  • Older adults have a true loss of muscle mass, which causes weakness.
  • The cell-level steps of contraction (sliding filaments, actin and myosin, calcium, ATP) are Not given in this file or Merck for this chapter.

F5. Developmental changes across the life span

Before birth to adulthood

  1. 3 months' gestation: the fetus has a "scale model" skeleton made of cartilage.
  2. Over the next months, the cartilage ossifies (turns into true bone) and grows.
  3. After birth: growth is fast in infancy, steady in childhood, then a rapid spurt in adolescence (boys and girls).
  4. Long bones grow wider as new bone is laid around the shaft.
  5. Long bones grow longer at the epiphyses (growth plates), discs at the bone ends.
  6. Trauma or infection at a growth plate can cause a bone deformity in a growing child.
  7. Growth in length stops when the epiphyses close. The last one closes at about age 20.

Spine curves develop in order

  1. Birth: one C-shaped curve (see Fig 23-49).
  2. 3 to 4 months: lifting the head while on the belly builds the cervical (neck) curve. (The infant exam page says a baby can lift the head prone by 2 months, which builds this curve and shows forearm strength.)
  3. 1 year to 18 months: standing up builds the lumbar curve.

Pregnancy ★ (see AP Q#20)

  1. Hormones rise: estrogen, relaxin (from the corpus luteum), and corticosteroids.
  2. These loosen the joints, mainly the sacroiliac, sacrococcygeal, and symphysis pubis joints of the pelvis.
  3. The growing baby shifts balance forward. The body adds progressive lordosis to shift weight back.
  4. This strains low back muscles: low back pain in late pregnancy.
  5. Other changes: anterior neck flexion (cervical flexion) and slumped shoulders (and kyphosis near the 3rd trimester).
  6. These upper-back changes can press on the ulnar and median nerves in the third trimester: aching, numbness, weakness in the arms.
  7. At term: "waddling" gait (Fig 23-53).

The aging adult ★ (see AP Q#19, Workbook SG7)

  • After peak bone mass, remodeling slowly tilts toward loss (see F1).
  • Long bones do NOT shorten. Height loss of 3 to 5 cm comes from the spine: discs lose water and get thinner, and osteoporosis shortens vertebrae.
  • Height loss is not significant until age 60. It is bigger in the 70s and 80s from collapsed vertebrae.
  • Expected posture changes: kyphosis, backward head tilt to make up for it, and slight flexion of hips and knees (the "figure 3" outline from the left side; see Fig 23-54).
  • Trunk gets shorter, so arms and legs look long ("lengthening of the arm-trunk axis").
  • Fat moves: in the 40s–50s, fat leaves the face and goes to the belly and hips. In the 80s–90s, less fat in the arms and legs (forearms) and more over belly and hips.
  • Less fat under the skin makes bony bumps stick out and body hollows deeper.
  • Real loss of muscle mass leads to weakness.
  • With no disease, ROM and strength are about the same as in younger adults.

K1. Getting ready (Preparation)

  1. Gather equipment: tape measure, skin marking pen, and sometimes a goniometer (a tool that measures joint angles).
  2. Wash hands.
  3. Make the person comfortable. Drape so you can see the body part without exposing more than needed.
  4. Use order: head to toe, proximal to distal (close to the trunk first, then out), from the midline outward.
  5. Support each joint at rest. Muscles must be soft and relaxed to feel the joint under them.
  6. Handle inflamed areas gently: firm support, gentle movement, gentle return to rest (rough moves cause pain and spasm).
  7. Compare paired joints (left vs right). Expect symmetry.
  8. Watch everyday motions as data: gait, posture, sitting, rising from a chair, taking off a jacket, handling a pen, rising from lying down.
  9. Choose the exam: a screening exam for most people; a complete exam for joint disease, past musculoskeletal symptoms, or ADL problems. Add age-specific screens (Ortolani for infants, scoliosis check for teens).

K2. The order for EVERY joint ★ (lab checklist, workbook Clinical Objective 2)

  1. Inspect: size and contour of the joint; skin color, swelling, masses, deformity. Compare with the other side. Swelling is significant: it signals joint irritation.
  2. Palpate: skin temperature, muscles, bones, joint capsule. Feel for heat, tenderness, swelling, masses. Joints are normally not tender. If tender, find the exact structure.
  3. Range of motion (ROM): ask for active ROM first (the person moves; you model it). If limited, gently try passive ROM (you move it with one hand anchoring the joint). ★ (see Workbook SG4)
  4. Muscle testing: repeat the motions while the person holds against your opposing force. Grade 0 to 5. Strength should be equal on both sides and fully resist you.

Memory chant: "Look, Feel, Move, Push." (Inspect, Palpate, ROM, Strength.)

Muscle strength grades: 5 = full ROM against gravity and full resistance (100%, normal). 4 = against gravity with some resistance (75%, good). 3 = against gravity only (50%, fair). 2 = only with gravity removed (25%, poor). 1 = slight contraction only (10%, trace). 0 = no contraction (0%, zero).

K3. Health history order (Subjective Data)

  1. Joints: pain (location, one side or both, quality, severity, onset, timing, what makes it worse or better, linked symptoms such as fever or sore throat), stiffness, swelling/heat/redness, tick bite, limited movement.
  2. Knee (if injured): how it happened, a "pop," can you stand on it, can you flex it.
  3. Muscles: pain or cramps (calf pain with walking?), weakness, smaller-looking muscles.
  4. Bones: pain, deformity, trauma (fracture, sprain, dislocation), back pain, numbness, limping.
  5. Functional assessment (ADLs): bathing, toileting, dressing, grooming, eating, mobility, communicating.
  6. Patient-centered care: job hazards, exercise, weight and diet (protein, calcium), medicines, supplements, effect of chronic illness on self and family, smoking and alcohol.
  7. Add age-specific questions (infant birth history and milestones; teen sports safety; older adult falls, mobility aids, DXA screening).

K4. TMJ (Fig 23-15, Fig 23-16)

  1. Person sits. Inspect the area in front of each ear.
  2. Place the tips of your first two fingers in front of each ear.
  3. Ask the person to open and close the mouth. Let your fingers sink into the dip over the joint. Feel for smooth motion. (A click or snap on opening is common in healthy people.)
  4. Ask them to open as wide as possible: normal gap 3 to 6 cm (3 fingers sideways).
  5. Ask them to open slightly, push the jaw forward, and move it side to side: 1 to 2 cm.
  6. Ask them to stick out the lower jaw: it should not drift to one side.
  7. Feel the temporalis and masseter while they clench. Compare size, firmness, strength.
  8. Have them move the jaw forward, sideways, and open against your resistance (tests cranial nerve V).

K5. Cervical spine (Fig 23-17)

  1. Inspect head and neck alignment: head upright, spine straight.
  2. Palpate the spinous processes and the sternomastoid, trapezius, and paravertebral muscles (firm, no spasm, not tender).
  3. Safety: do NOT do neck ROM if neck trauma is suspected.
  4. Chin to chest: flexion 45°.
  5. Chin to ceiling: hyperextension 55°.
  6. Ear to shoulder without lifting the shoulder: lateral bending 40°.
  7. Chin toward each shoulder: rotation 70°.
  8. Repeat against your resistance. Holding flexion against resistance also tests cranial nerve XI.

K6. Shoulder (Fig 23-18)

  1. Inspect both shoulders front and back: size, contour, equal bony landmarks. Look for redness, atrophy, deformity, swelling.
  2. If there is pain, have the person point to the spot with the hand on the good side.
  3. Standing in front, palpate both shoulders in this order: clavicle → AC joint → scapula → greater tubercle → subacromial bursa area → biceps groove → front of the glenohumeral joint. Then palpate the axilla.
  4. Cup a hand over the shoulder during ROM to feel for crepitus.
  5. Arms forward and up: forward flexion 180°; back: hyperextension up to 50°.
  6. Hands behind the back toward the scapulae: internal rotation 90°.
  7. Arms out to the sides and up, palms meeting overhead: abduction 180°; across the body: adduction 50°.
  8. Hands behind the head, elbows back: external rotation 90°.
  9. Strength: shrug (tests CN XI), flex forward and up, and abduct against resistance.
  10. Advanced (rotator cuff tear): drop arm test: passively abduct the arm; positive if the person cannot hold it up and hitches the shoulder.

K7. Elbow (Fig 23-19, Fig 23-20, Fig 23-21, Fig 23-22)

  1. Inspect in flexed and extended positions: size, contour, deformity, redness, swelling. Check the olecranon bursa and the hollows beside the olecranon.
  2. Flex the elbow to about 70°, relaxed. Support the forearm with your hand.
  3. Palpate the olecranon and the medial and lateral epicondyles with your other hand.
  4. Thumb in the lateral groove, index and middle fingers in the medial groove; palpate both sides of the olecranon with varied pressure.
  5. Palpate the olecranon bursa area for heat, swelling, tenderness, nodules. Repeat on the other arm.
  6. Bend and straighten: flexion 150–160°, extension 0° (some lack 5–10°, some hyperextend 5–10°).
  7. Forearm on the table: turn palm up and palm down (supination and pronation).
  8. Strength: stabilize the arm; person flexes, then extends, against your resistance just above the wrist.
  9. After a fall: full elbow extension usually rules out a fracture.

K8. Wrist and hand (Fig 23-23 to Fig 23-27)

  1. Inspect back and palm of both hands: position, contour, shape, skin, thenar eminence.
  2. Palpate the wrist: support the hand from below, press the dorsum with both thumbs, slide side to side to find the joint dips.
  3. Palpate each MCP joint with your thumbs, just distal to and on each side of the knuckle.
  4. Palpate the sides of each PIP and DIP joint with a thumb-and-index pinch.
  5. ROM: bend hand up (hyperextension 70°), down (palmar flexion 90°); fingers at MCP (flexion 90°, hyperextension 30°); palms flat, turn out and in (ulnar 50–60°, radial 20°); spread fingers (abduction 20°); make a fist; touch thumb to each finger and the base of the little finger.
  6. Strength: forearm supinated on the table, hold mid-forearm, person flexes the wrist against your resistance at the palm. Also test finger extension, spread, and grip (per lab sheet).
  7. Special tests for carpal tunnel: Phalen and Tinel (K9).

K9. Phalen test and Tinel sign (Fig 23-28, Fig 23-29)

Phalen test

  1. Ask the person to put the backs of both hands together, fingers pointing down.
  2. Wrists are flexed 90°.
  3. Hold for 60 seconds.
  4. Normal: no symptoms. Positive: numbness and burning (carpal tunnel syndrome).

Tinel sign

  1. Find the median nerve at the wrist.
  2. Tap (percuss) directly over it.
  3. Normal: no symptoms. Positive: burning and tingling along the median nerve.

Memory trick: "Tinel = Tap. Phalen = Push the backs of the hands together (Pray upside down)."

Ⓜ Merck A third test, the median nerve compression test, causes symptoms within 30 seconds. Nerve conduction studies confirm the diagnosis.

K10. Hip (Fig 23-30)

  1. Inspect the hip later, with the spine, while the person stands: iliac crests level, gluteal folds even, buttocks equal. A smooth gait suggests equal leg length and good hip function.
  2. Person supine: palpate both hips (stable, even, no tenderness or crepitus).
  3. Raise each straight leg: hip flexion 90°.
  4. Knee to chest, other leg flat: hip flexion 120°. The other thigh must stay on the table (see Thomas test, K11).
  5. Flex hip and knee to 90°. Hold the thigh and ankle. Swing the foot out, then in: internal rotation 40°, external rotation 45°. Limited internal rotation = early, reliable sign of hip disease.
  6. Steady the pelvis (press down on the opposite anterior superior iliac spine). Swing the straight leg out, then in: abduction 40–45°, adduction 20–30°. Limited abduction while supine = the most common motion problem in hip disease.
  7. Later, standing (or prone, or bent over the table): swing the straight leg back: hyperextension 15° with the pelvis held still.

K11. Thomas test (hip flexion deformity)

  1. Person lies supine.
  2. Bring one knee up to the chest; keep the other leg straight on the table.
  3. Bending the hip flattens the lumbar spine.
  4. Watch the opposite (straight) leg. Normal: the thigh stays down on the table.
  5. Positive Thomas test: the opposite thigh lifts off the table, which shows a flexion deformity of that hip.

K12. Trendelenburg test

Not given in this file or Merck. The file and the Merck pages used do not describe a Trendelenburg test or sign. Ask your instructor whether it is part of your lab checkoff.

K13. Knee (Fig 23-31 to Fig 23-36)

  1. Person supine, legs straight (some examiners use knees bent and dangling).
  2. Inspect skin, alignment (lower leg in line with the thigh), the hollows beside the patella, prepatellar bursa, suprapatellar pouch, and the quadriceps (atrophy shows first in the inner part, the vastus medialis).
  3. With the quadriceps relaxed, start about 10 cm above the patella. Grasp with thumb and fingers and move down, exploring the suprapatellar pouch.
  4. If there is swelling, decide: soft-tissue swelling or extra fluid? Use the bulge sign (small amount) or ballottement (large amount) (K14).
  5. Palpate the tibiofemoral joint line, the infrapatellar fat pad, and the patella.
  6. Hold the patella while the knee flexes and extends to feel for crepitus.
  7. ROM standing: flexion 130–150°; extension 0° (hyperextension 15° in some people).
  8. Watch ROM during walking. Optional: squat and "duck walk" if able.
  9. Strength: person holds the knee flexed while you pull the leg forward. Extension strength = rising from a low chair or a squat without hands.
  10. If injury with locking, giving way, or local pain: McMurray test (K15) and the Ottawa knee rules (K16).

K14. Bulge sign and ballottement (Fig 23-32, Fig 23-33)

Bulge sign (small effusions)

  1. Firmly stroke UP the medial (inner) side of the knee 2 to 3 times to push fluid away.
  2. Tap the lateral (outer) side.
  3. Watch the medial hollow.
  4. Positive: a distinct bulge (fluid wave) returns. It shows with as little as 4 to 8 mL of fluid.

Ballottement of the patella (larger effusions)

  1. Left hand squeezes the suprapatellar pouch to push fluid into the joint.
  2. Right hand pushes the patella sharply against the femur.
  3. Normal: the patella is already snug; no movement.
  4. Positive: the patella moves through fluid and you feel a tap as it bumps the femoral condyles.

K15. McMurray test (torn meniscus) (Fig 23-36)

  1. Person supine. Stand on the affected side.
  2. Hold the heel. Flex the knee and hip.
  3. Other hand on the knee, fingers on the medial side.
  4. Rotate the leg in and out to loosen the joint.
  5. Push the leg into external rotation and apply a valgus (inward) stress to the knee.
  6. Slowly extend the knee.
  7. Positive: a click you hear or feel = torn meniscus → refer to orthopedics.

K16. Ottawa rules (when to x-ray)

Knee (after injury): image if ANY of these

  1. Pain only at the patella or at the head of the fibula.
  2. Age 55 or older.
  3. Cannot flex the knee to 90°.
  4. Cannot bear weight for 4 steps.

Ankle or midfoot (after injury)

  1. Palpate the full length of the medial and lateral ankle ligaments (position of Fig 23-38).
  2. X-ray is needed if there is pain near the malleoli or midfoot AND either inability to bear weight for 4 steps or bone tenderness. (The full printed box did not scan.)

K17. Ankle and foot (Fig 23-37 to Fig 23-40)

  1. Inspect sitting (no weight), then standing and walking. Foot lines up with the leg (mid-patella to between toes 1 and 2). Weight runs heel → midfoot → between toes 2 and 3.
  2. Look at toes, arch, malleoli, skin, calluses, and the wear on old shoes.
  3. Support the ankle by holding the heel; palpate the joint spaces with your thumbs.
  4. Palpate each MTP joint between thumb (top) and fingers (sole).
  5. Pinch the sides of each toe IP joint.
  6. ROM: toes to floor = plantar flexion 45°; toes to nose = dorsiflexion 20°.
  7. Hold the heel and steady the ankle (tests the subtalar joint): soles out = eversion 20°; soles in = inversion 30°.
  8. Flex and straighten the toes.
  9. Strength: hold dorsiflexion and plantar flexion against your resistance.

K18. Spine (thoracic and lumbar) (Fig 23-41, Fig 23-42, Fig 23-43)

  1. Person stands, gown open in back. Stand far enough back to see the whole back.
  2. Check a straight vertical line from the head through the spinous processes to the gluteal cleft.
  3. Check equal levels of shoulders, scapulae, iliac crests, gluteal folds, and equal spaces between arms and trunk.
  4. Knees and feet line up and point forward.
  5. Side view: convex thoracic curve, concave lumbar curve.
  6. Palpate the spinous processes (straight, not tender) and paravertebral muscles (firm, no spasm).
  7. Bend forward and touch toes: flexion 75–90°. The lumbar curve should flatten into one C-shaped curve.
  8. If a curve is suspected: while bent, mark a dot on each spinous process. When standing, the dots should form a straight line (an S-shape = curve).
  9. Steady the pelvis. Bend back: hyperextension 30°. Bend sideways: 35°. Twist shoulders each way: rotation 30°.
  10. Walk a few steps on the toes, then back on the heels.

K19. Scoliosis screening: forward bend test ★★ (see AP Q#23)

Workbook correct order (key: 3, 2, 5, 7):

  1. ★ Position yourself BEHIND the client so the full spine is visible. (workbook step 3)
  2. ★ Note the level of shoulders, scapulae, and iliac crests. (workbook step 2)
  3. ★ Ask the client to bend at the waist and reach for the toes. (workbook step 5)
  4. ★ Note the level of shoulders, ribs, and iliac crests while they are bent forward. (workbook step 7)

Wrong steps (not used): step 1 (standing in FRONT), step 4 ("ribs and superior iliac spine" while standing), step 6 ("superior iliac spine" while bent). Trap: the distractors swap "iliac crests" for "superior iliac spine" and put you in front.

Textbook detail: sit behind the teen; feet shoulder-width apart; slowly bend to touch the toes. Expect a straight spine standing and bending, symmetric posterior ribs, and equal shoulders, scapulae, iliac crests. Positive = a rib hump on one side when bending (Table 23-7 photo a). Note: the USPSTF does NOT support routine screening of teens with no symptoms, but you must be ready to check when a curve is found or the family is worried.

K20. Straight leg raising (Lasègue test) (Fig 23-44)

  1. Person lies supine.
  2. Keep the knee straight. Raise the affected leg to just short of the point of pain.
  3. Dorsiflex the foot.
  4. Positive: reproduces or worsens sciatic pain and the person resists more lifting. Pain that increases with dorsiflexion points even more strongly to a herniated disc.
  5. Then raise the unaffected leg (other leg flat) and ask about pain on the involved side. If this reproduces the sciatic pain, it strongly suggests a herniated nucleus pulposus.
  6. Note: SLR is the most sensitive test to tell sciatica from nonspecific back pain. Slump and Bowstring tests are sometimes added.

K21. Leg length measurement ★ (see Workbook SG5) (Fig 23-45)

True leg length

  1. Person lies supine with legs straight.
  2. Measure from a fixed point, the anterior (superior) iliac spine,
  3. to another fixed point, the medial malleolus,
  4. with the tape crossing the medial side of the knee.
  5. Repeat on the other leg. Normal: equal or within 1 cm.

Apparent leg length (when true lengths are equal but the legs LOOK unequal)

  1. Measure from a nonfixed point, the umbilicus,
  2. to the medial malleolus on each leg.
  3. True equal but apparent unequal = pelvic tilt (obliquity) or an adduction or flexion deformity of the hip.

K22. Infant exam (Fig 23-46 to Fig 23-50)

  1. Undress fully; infant on the back. Use a warming table for newborns.
  2. Start at the feet and move up.
  3. Feet: is the position flexible or fixed? Scratch the outer sole (a flexible foot returns to a right angle), or hold the heel and push the forefoot to neutral.
  4. Check forefoot vs hindfoot (metatarsus adductus vs varus vs clubfoot).
  5. Tibial torsion: feet flat, knees bent, patella and tibial tubercle in line, fingers on the malleoli. The line through the four malleoli should be parallel to the table (abnormal if over 20° off, or lateral malleolus in front of medial).
  6. Hips: Ortolani at every visit until age 1 (K23). Then Allis test and gluteal folds.
  7. Hands and arms: shape, number of fingers, palm creases.
  8. Clavicles: palpate the whole length (most often broken at birth). Watch for equal arm motion during the Moro reflex.
  9. Back: lift the infant; check the C-curve; look for a tuft of hair, dimple, cyst, or mass.
  10. Watch spontaneous arm and leg movement for ROM.
  11. Strength: lift with your hands under the axillae. Normal: the baby wedges securely. Slipping through = shoulder weakness.

K23. Ortolani maneuver ★ (see Workbook SG6) (Fig 23-46)

  1. Infant supine, knees flexed.
  2. Place your thumbs on the inner mid-thighs and your fingers on the outer hips over the greater trochanters.
  3. Adduct the legs until your thumbs touch.
  4. Gently lift and abduct: move the knees apart and down until the outer sides touch the table.
  5. Normal: smooth, no sound.
  6. Positive: a "clunk" as the femoral head pops back into the socket → refer.

K24. Barlow maneuver (Merck only)

  1. Ⓜ Merck Gently adduct the hip (knee drawn across the body).
  2. Ⓜ Merck Push the thigh posteriorly (backward).
  3. Ⓜ Merck A clunk means the femoral head is sliding OUT of the socket.

The file does not describe Barlow. Not in the textbook pages; Merck only.

K25. Allis test / Galeazzi sign (Fig 23-47)

  1. Baby supine, knees flexed, feet flat on the table.
  2. Compare the height of the knees.
  3. Normal: same height. Positive Allis sign: one knee clearly lower → suggests hip dislocation.
  4. Ⓜ Merck The same finding is called the Galeazzi sign; the knee is lower on the affected side.

K26. Young children (preschool and school age)

  1. Watch spontaneous play first. Coax motions ("walk to Mom," hop, jump).
  2. Back: plumb line from the back of the head to the middle of the sacrum; shoulders level within 1 cm.
  3. Legs from the front: measure knee gap (varum) or ankle gap (valgum).
  4. Watch the child walk away and back in socks. Check shoe wear.
  5. Ages 2–6: start with feet and hands (less scary), then proceed as for adults.
  6. Check the arm for full ROM; look for radial head subluxation.

K27. Older adult: Get Up and Go test

  1. Person sits in an armchair. Start timing.
  2. Person rises from the chair.
  3. Walks 10 feet.
  4. Turns around.
  5. Walks back.
  6. Sits down. Stop timing.
  7. Healthy adult over 60: under 12 seconds. 12 seconds or more = higher fall risk.

Non-modifiableModifiable
  • Advanced age, female sex, history of fracture = main risks in all racial/ethnic groups

  • Smaller height and weight; younger age at menopause; low estrogen

  • Peak BMD timing: women peak earlier than men

H2. Race and culture notes (from the Genetics and Environment box)

  • Non-Hispanic Black adults: higher BMD and slower bone loss than all other groups.
  • BUT Black women and men have higher death rates after hip fracture and longer hospital stays.
  • White women in the US are more likely than Black, Hispanic, and Asian peers to get osteoporosis.
  • Asian and White women have similar BMD, but Asian women have lower rates of osteoporosis.
  • Hispanic population: BMD varies by country of origin; more research needed.
  • Low BMD is a strong, steady predictor of hip and vertebral fractures.
  • Teach prevention to all women, no matter their race or ethnicity.
  • Ⓜ Merck Non-Hispanic White and Asian women are listed as higher risk, but Merck notes the differences are complex and shaped by social factors.

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) (LO6; see Workbook SG8, Q#21, Q#22)
Pathophysiology (cell to symptom)
  1. The immune system attacks the body's own joint tissue. This is called autoimmune.
  2. The synovium (joint lining) becomes inflamed. Its cells multiply too much (hyperplasia), so the lining swells and thickens. It feels "boggy."
  3. Immune chemical signals (cytokines, chemokines) and tissue-eating enzymes (metalloproteases) drive the damage Ⓜ Merck.
  4. The swollen lining turns into tough scar-like tissue (fibrosis). It wears away cartilage and bone (erosions, cysts).
  5. Damaged capsules stretch and muscles pull unevenly. Fingers drift toward the pinky side (ulnar drift), and swan-neck and boutonniere shapes form.
  6. The inflammation is body-wide. This causes tiredness, low fever, poor appetite and weight loss.
Etiology (cause)
Autoimmune. The exact trigger is not given in this file. About 2.5 times more common in women. Peak age 30 to 60, but any age. Merck says women are affected 2 to 3 times more, with onset most often at 35 to 50.
Signs (objective)
  • Symmetric, bilateral joints (both sides the same) with heat, redness, swelling.
  • Boggy, tender MCP joints (this does not happen in OA).
  • Fusiform (spindle-shaped) swelling of the PIP joints; warm hands; puffed-up veins.
  • Ulnar deviation, swan-neck, boutonniere deformity, wrist subluxation.
  • Rheumatoid nodules: firm bumps the skin slides over, at the elbow (olecranon) and along the back edge of the forearm (ulna).
  • Ankylosis: wrist stuck in extreme flexion from torn extensor tendons.
  • Crepitation (grating) from roughened joint surfaces; hallux valgus is common in RA.
  • Low-grade fever, swollen lymph nodes, weight loss, muscle wasting (for example the small hand muscles in AP Q#21).
Symptoms (subjective)
  • Pain worst in the morning on waking; movement makes it better (opposite of most joint pain).
  • Stiffness in the morning and after rest. Case M.T. had morning stiffness of ½ to 1 hour. Merck says morning stiffness often lasts more than 60 minutes.
  • Fatigue, weakness, poor appetite (anorexia).
  • Possible feelings of low self-worth, body image worry, loss of independence.
Diagnostics / Labs
  • File: diagnosis is from the history and exam pattern. No lab values given.
  • Rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-CCP antibody blood tests support RA Ⓜ Merck.
  • ESR and CRP (blood tests that rise with inflammation) help show the arthritis is inflammatory, but they are not specific Ⓜ Merck.
  • Classification counts the number of swollen joints, the antibody results, ESR/CRP, and symptoms lasting 6 weeks or more. DIP joints are left out of the count Ⓜ Merck.
  • Imaging can show erosions Ⓜ Merck.
Risk
To develop it: female sex, age 30 to 60 (file). Complications: joint deformity and loss of function; long-term pain even when inflammation is controlled; higher risk of heart attack and stroke; RA is also a FRAX risk factor for broken bones.
Expected treatment
  • File: anti-inflammatory drugs; aspirin for flare-ups (case M.T.); rest periods; daily exercises.
  • DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) such as methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide; biologic drugs such as TNF inhibitors; JAK inhibitors Ⓜ Merck.
  • Steroid shots into a joint; physical measures; sometimes surgery Ⓜ Merck.
Prognosis
Chronic with flare-ups (M.T. flared every 6 to 8 months). DMARDs can reduce symptoms and slow damage Ⓜ Merck.
Nursing interventions (independent, with rationale)
  • Check both sides and compare. Why: a matching both-sides pattern points to RA.
  • Support the joint and move it gently, then return it to rest. Why: rough handling of an inflamed joint causes pain and muscle spasm.
  • Teach a balance of rest periods and daily exercise. Why: M.T.'s nursing assessment listed both needs; movement eases RA stiffness.
  • Teach how to take aspirin as ordered, and not to cut the dose on her own. Why: M.T. lowered her own dose once she felt better.
  • Ask about stomach pain and bleeding. Why: aspirin and NSAIDs can cause GI pain and bleeding.
  • Do a functional (ADL) check: buttoning, opening jars, writing. Why: hand deformity causes self-care deficits.
  • Ask how the illness affects family, friends and self-view. Why: screens for body image change, role change and social isolation.
Osteoarthritis (OA), also called Degenerative Joint Disease (LO6; see Workbook SG9)
Pathophysiology
  1. The cartilage cushion at the bone ends slowly breaks down.
  2. The bone just under the cartilage (subchondral bone) reshapes itself.
  3. New bone grows at the joint edges as osteophytes (bone spurs). Bits of cartilage may float loose ("loose bodies").
  4. There is only a little synovial inflammation, so OA is called noninflammatory.
  5. Rough surfaces grind (crepitus) and motion becomes painful and limited. Spurs on finger joints show as hard nodes.
Etiology
Wear and repair imbalance in a joint. Linked to older age, female sex, obesity, genetics, and major joint injury.
Signs
  • Asymmetric (not matching on both sides). Common in hands, knees, hips, low back and neck.
  • Hard, nontender, noninflammatory nodes 2 to 3 mm or larger: Heberden = DIP, Bouchard = PIP (less common).
  • Swelling with hard bony bumps; limited motion.
  • Knee: uneven bony edges, pain along the joint line, marked crepitus. A positive bulge sign marks people at high risk for worsening knee OA.
  • MCP joints are not boggy (that is RA).
  • Merck adds common sites: thumb base (CMC), big toe joint (1st MTP).
Symptoms
Stiffness; pain with motion; pain worse later in the day. Ⓜ Merck stiffness lasts less than 30 minutes after waking or sitting still; pain is worse with weight bearing and better with rest.
Diagnostics / Labs
X-ray findings may not match how bad the symptoms are (file). Ⓜ Merck diagnosis is usually by x-ray. No lab test is given in the file.
Risk
To develop it: age, female sex, obesity, genetics, joint injury; a torn meniscus raises later knee OA risk. Complications: loss of function, falls (case L.M.'s knee "gave out" on the stairs), weakened quadriceps.
Expected treatment
  • File: case L.M. used acetaminophen daily and still had pain.
  • First line is non-drug care: education, weight loss if needed, exercise for strength and flexibility, supports and braces Ⓜ Merck.
  • Drugs are add-ons: topical NSAIDs first for knees and hands; oral NSAIDs for the shortest time; duloxetine; acetaminophen has limited benefit Ⓜ Merck.
  • Steroid shots into the joint give short-term relief; frequent shots may speed cartilage loss. Opioids are generally avoided Ⓜ Merck.
  • Joint replacement or other surgery if other care fails Ⓜ Merck.
Prognosis
Progressive (slowly gets worse). No other outlook given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask when pain is worst. Why: pain later in the day fits OA; morning pain fits RA.
  • Test quadriceps strength by rising from a low chair without hands. Why: weak quads make the knee give way.
  • Screen fall risk and teach home safety. Why: a buckling knee and stairs led to a near fall in case L.M.
  • Review every pain medicine and ask about stomach pain or bleeding. Why: daily pain pills, NSAIDs and aspirin need safety checks.
  • Support weight control and regular exercise. Why: obesity is a risk factor, and exercise is first-line care Ⓜ Merck.
Osteoporosis (LO5, LO6, LO7; see AP FRAX case)
Pathophysiology
  1. Bone is always being broken down (resorption) and rebuilt (deposition). This is called remodeling.
  2. Peak bone mass is reached in the mid-20s.
  3. Later, breakdown runs faster than rebuilding. Bone mineral density (BMD) drops, and bone becomes porous and weak.
  4. Less estrogen speeds this up, because bone-building cells (osteoblasts) have estrogen receptors.
  5. Too little calcium in the diet makes the body release extra parathyroid hormone, which pulls calcium out of bone.
  6. Weak bones break easily: wrist, hip, vertebrae. Collapsed vertebrae cause height loss and kyphosis.
Etiology
Bone loss faster than bone gain. Mostly postmenopausal White women. Also linked to small body size, early menopause, inactivity, low estrogen. Obesity may be related.
Signs
Kyphosis, height loss (more than the expected 3 to 5 cm), fractures. Merck calls the upper back hump a "dowager hump."
Symptoms
Often no symptoms until a bone breaks Ⓜ Merck. Low back pain can occur with osteoporosis (file).
Diagnostics / Labs
  • DXA (DEXA) scan: a low-dose x-ray of bone density. Quick, noninvasive, low radiation.
  • FRAX: a computer tool from the World Health Organization. It gives the 10-year chance of a hip fracture and of a major osteoporotic fracture. Inputs: age (40–90), sex, weight, height, past fracture, parent with hip fracture, current smoking, glucocorticoids, RA, secondary osteoporosis, 3+ alcohol units a day, femoral neck BMD.
  • Serum vitamin D level can be checked.
  • Low BMD is a strong, steady predictor of hip and spine fractures.
Risk
To develop it: advanced age, female sex, past fracture (all groups); White women more than Black, Hispanic, Asian peers; smoking; heavy alcohol; glucocorticoid use; RA; parent with hip fracture; low body weight; inactivity. Complications: fragility fractures. Black women and men have higher death rates and longer hospital stays after hip fracture, even though their fracture risk is lower.
Expected treatment
  • Bisphosphonates are first-line (file and Merck).
  • Hormone therapy is not recommended because of its risks (file). Merck lists hormone therapy among possible drugs; follow the file for the exam.
  • Calcium and vitamin D; strength and balance exercise; quit smoking; limit alcohol.
  • Other drugs: denosumab, teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab Ⓜ Merck.
Prognosis
Bisphosphonates can cut spine and hip fractures by up to 50% Ⓜ Merck.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask about DXA screening and results. Why: women 65 and older should be screened.
  • Teach calcium and vitamin D goals and food sources (dairy, fortified juice and cereal, soy milk; read the % daily value). Why: food calcium is absorbed better than pills.
  • Teach strength, balance and brisk walking. Why: activity slows bone loss and lowers fracture risk.
  • Teach fall prevention: better lighting, safe stairs, remove throw rugs, grab bars, yearly vision and hearing checks, review medicines that affect balance. Why: falls cause the fractures.
  • Help with quitting smoking or vaping; limit alcohol. Why: both lower BMD; alcohol also raises fall risk.
  • Measure height at each visit. Why: extra height loss can signal spine collapse.
Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS) (LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. Long-term inflammation of the vertebrae (spondylitis).
  2. In severe cases, the spine joints fuse into solid bone (ankylosis).
  3. Muscle spasm beside the spine pulls the body forward. The neck and low back curves flatten. The upper back rounds into one big kyphosis.
  4. Hips and knees bend to make up for it.
Etiology
Cause unknown; genetic risk. More common in males. Starts slowly, so diagnosis is often late.
Signs
Forward-flexed posture, one long kyphotic curve, limited spine ROM.
Symptoms
Inflammatory back pain: dull, deep, low back or buttocks; morning stiffness 30 minutes or more that gets better with activity; waking at night with pain; onset at age 45 or younger. (Merck lists onset at 40 or younger as an inflammatory back pain feature.)
Diagnostics / Labs
File: none given. Ⓜ Merck sacroiliac joint inflammation (sacroiliitis) on x-ray or MRI; ESR, CRP, CBC; HLA-B27 gene test.
Risk
To develop it: male sex, genetics. Complications (file): thin bones (osteopenia), fragility fractures, nerve problems such as cauda equina syndrome. Merck adds eye inflammation (anterior uveitis), aortitis and heart rhythm problems.
Expected treatment
NSAIDs first line; TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors or JAK inhibitors; exercises to keep the spine flexible Ⓜ Merck.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask: does back pain improve with activity, and does it wake you at night? Why: these point to inflammatory back pain, not a strain.
  • Measure spine ROM and watch posture over time. Why: limited spine ROM occurs with AS.
  • Teach fall and fracture safety. Why: thin bones and a stiff spine raise fracture risk.
  • Report new bladder or bowel changes right away. Why: cauda equina syndrome is a listed complication.
Gout (acute gout and chronic gout with tophi) (LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. Too much uric acid in the blood.
  2. Uric acid forms sharp urate crystals in the joint space.
  3. The crystals strongly trigger inflammation: a sudden, very painful attack.
  4. With years of gout, crystal lumps called tophi build up in and around joints, causing swelling and deformity.
Etiology
High blood uric acid. Attacks are triggered by surgery, trauma, diuretics (water pills) and alcohol.
Signs
Red, swollen, warm joint, often the big toe joint (1st MTP). Tophi = hard nodules, most often at the 1st MTP. Ⓜ Merck tophi also on fingers, hands, elbow, Achilles tendon, ears. Elbow bursitis and elbow arthritis can be caused by gout.
Symptoms
Extreme, continuous, throbbing pain. Ⓜ Merck early attacks usually hit one joint and go away by themselves in 7 to 10 days.
Diagnostics / Labs
File: none given. Ⓜ Merck joint fluid (synovial fluid) analysis to see crystals; dual-energy CT or ultrasound can help.
Risk
To develop it: obesity, metabolic syndrome, high blood pressure, high blood fats. Complications: tophi, joint deformity, chronic deforming arthritis; uric acid kidney stones Ⓜ Merck.
Expected treatment
File: responds well to medicine. Ⓜ Merck stop attacks with NSAIDs, colchicine or glucocorticoids; lower uric acid long term with allopurinol or febuxostat; treat blood pressure, blood fats and weight; avoid high-purine foods.
Prognosis
Gout responds well to medication (file). Without uric acid-lowering care, attacks come closer together and tophi form Ⓜ Merck.
Nursing interventions
  • Handle the joint very gently; keep bed linen off the toe if it hurts. Why: even light touch can be extremely painful (general comfort measure; not stated in the file).
  • Ask about recent surgery, injury, water pills and alcohol. Why: these trigger attacks.
  • Teach alcohol limits and weight control. Why: alcohol triggers attacks; obesity raises risk.
  • Inspect the toes, ears and elbows for hard lumps. Why: tophi mean long-standing gout.
Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS) (LO4, LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. The median nerve runs through a tight tunnel at the wrist (the carpal tunnel).
  2. Repeated motion or swelling squeezes the nerve.
  3. A squeezed nerve sends burning, tingling and numbness to the thumb, index and middle fingers.
  4. Over time the nerve cannot run the thumb muscles well. The thumb mound (thenar eminence) wastes away (atrophy).
Etiology
Chronic repetitive motion. Age 40 to 60, more in women. Ⓜ Merck most common in women 40 to 59; also linked to RA, diabetes, low thyroid, pregnancy swelling. Pregnancy posture in the third trimester can press on the median and ulnar nerves (file).
Signs
Positive Phalen test (wrists bent 90° back to back for 60 seconds brings on numbness and burning). Positive Tinel sign (tapping the median nerve brings burning and tingling along it). Thenar atrophy.
Symptoms
Pain, night pain, burning, numbness in the thumb, index and middle finger. Ⓜ Merck wakes the person at night; they shake the hand to get relief.
Diagnostics / Labs
File: Phalen and Tinel. Ⓜ Merck median nerve compression test (symptoms within 30 seconds); nerve conduction study confirms.
Risk
To develop it: jobs with repetitive motion (the history asks about this), women, age 40 to 60. Complications: weak grip and thumb wasting.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck wrist splint, better work set-up (ergonomics), steroid injection, surgery.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask about work and hobbies with repeated wrist motion. Why: the history screens occupational risk for CTS.
  • Compare both thenar mounds. Why: one flat mound = late nerve damage.
  • Teach splint use and work changes as ordered. Why: these reduce pressure on the nerve Ⓜ Merck.
Rotator Cuff Tear (and tendinitis) (LO4, LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. Four muscles and tendons (SITS) hold the ball of the arm bone in the shallow socket.
  2. A tendon tears from injury, or wears out from overuse or pinching under the acromion Ⓜ Merck.
  3. Without that tendon, the arm cannot be lifted out to the side and held there.
  4. The person shrugs or hitches the shoulder to use the muscles that still work.
Etiology
Forceful pull-in while the arm is held out (traumatic adduction during abduction); falling on the shoulder; throwing; heavy lifting. Ⓜ Merck the supraspinatus tendon is involved most often.
Signs
"Hunched" shoulder; limited abduction; positive drop arm test (you lift the arm out to the side and the person cannot hold it). Shoulder atrophy can mean a rotator cuff problem. Rotator cuff lesions limit abduction while forward flexion stays fairly normal.
Symptoms
Pain and muscle spasm when lifting the arm to the side. Ⓜ Merck shoulder pain; weakness with severe tears.
Diagnostics / Labs
Imaging is needed to confirm (file). Ⓜ Merck MRI and/or arthroscopy (camera in the joint) for some patients.
Risk
To develop it: throwing sports, heavy lifting, falls. Complications: loss of shoulder motion, muscle wasting.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck rest, NSAIDs, rotator cuff strengthening exercises; sometimes a steroid shot under the acromion (repeat shots can weaken tendons); surgery for a complete tear.
Prognosis
Most tendinitis and partial tears do well without surgery Ⓜ Merck.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask the person to point to the pain with the good hand. Why: it locates the pain without moving the sore arm.
  • Remember shoulder pain may come from the heart, lungs or a hiatal hernia. Why: pain that you cannot reproduce by pressing or moving may be referred and serious.
  • Cup your hand over the shoulder during ROM. Why: you can feel crepitus.

Pathophysiology
Scar-like fibrous tissue forms in the joint capsule. The capsule tightens. Motion is lost little by little.
Etiology
Gradual onset. Linked to diabetes (20% of people with diabetes have it), long bed rest, or keeping the shoulder still. Ⓜ Merck holding a shoulder injury completely still can quickly lead to frozen shoulder.
Signs
Limited abduction and external rotation; cannot reach overhead; may cause shoulder girdle muscle atrophy.
Symptoms
Stiffness, pain, especially one-sided night pain.
Diagnostics / Labs
Not given in this file or Merck pages used (diagnosed by exam pattern in the file).
Risk
Diabetes, immobility. Complication: muscle wasting.
Expected treatment
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask patients with diabetes about shoulder stiffness and night pain. Why: 1 in 5 people with diabetes has it.
  • Encourage safe shoulder movement during long bed rest. Why: immobility is a cause.
Dislocated Shoulder (Glenohumeral Dislocation) (LO6)
Pathophysiology
The ball of the arm bone pops out of the shallow socket. 95% go forward (anterior). The ligaments are torn, so it can happen again.
Etiology
Trauma with the arm out, back and rotated: falling on an outstretched arm, diving into a pool. It is the most common joint dislocation.
Signs
Shoulder loses its round shape and looks flat on the side; looks hunched forward; clavicle tip looks out of place; obvious deformity. Ⓜ Merck acromion sticks out; elbow held a little away from the body; person will not move the arm.
Symptoms
Severe pain.
Diagnostics / Labs
X-rays (radiography) needed (file). Ⓜ Merck specific AP and axillary x-ray views show the ball outside the socket.
Risk
Complications: repeat dislocation (file). Ⓜ Merck nerve injury (axillary nerve, brachial plexus), rotator cuff tear (older adults), fracture; repeat dislocation common at age 40 or younger.
Expected treatment
Closed reduction (putting it back without surgery) is most common (file). Ⓜ Merck then a sling and swathe right away.
Prognosis
Risk of repeat dislocation because the ligaments were hurt.
Nursing interventions
  • Check feeling over the deltoid and movement of the hand before and after reduction. Why: the axillary nerve can be hurt; it numbs the outer shoulder Ⓜ Merck.
  • Do not force the arm through ROM. Why: deformity after trauma needs an x-ray first.
  • Teach sling use as ordered. Why: it protects the joint after reduction Ⓜ Merck.

A round fluid-filled sac (cyst) grows from a tendon sheath or joint capsule. If large or in a tight spot, it can press on a nerve.
Etiology
Not given in this file. Ⓜ Merck dorsal wrist is the usual site (about 65%).
Signs
Round, nontender lump, usually on the back (dorsum) of the wrist; easier to see when the wrist is bent forward. Ⓜ Merck light shines through it (transilluminates).
Symptoms

Exam (file). Merck adds transillumination.
Risk
Most common in females 20 to 40 (file). Merck says adults 20 to 50, women 3 times more than men.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck may go away alone; draining with a needle (aspiration) or surgical removal if bothersome.

Dupuytren Contracture (LO6)
Pathophysiology
The tough tissue sheet in the palm (palmar fascia) slowly thickens and overgrows (hyperplasia). Cords form from mid-palm to the fingers. The cords shorten and pull the fingers into a bent position. Order: 4th finger first, then 5th, then 3rd. Ⓜ Merck often starts as a lump (nodule) in the palm.
Etiology
Inherited tendency; linked to diabetes, epilepsy and alcohol-related liver disease. Men over 40 (Merck says over 45).
Signs
Bent (flexion contracture) fingers; puckered palm skin; usually both hands.
Symptoms
Painless, but it gets in the way of hand use.
Diagnostics / Labs
Exam. Ⓜ Merck surgery is usually needed when the hand cannot lie flat on a table.
Risk
Men, family history, diabetes, epilepsy, alcohol use. Complication: loss of hand function.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck steroid shot into an early nodule; surgery (needle fasciotomy or removing the tissue); collagenase enzyme injection.
Prognosis
Slowly progressive Ⓜ Merck.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask the person to lay the hand flat on a table. Why: a quick check of how bad it is and when to refer Ⓜ Merck.
  • Check ADLs that need an open hand (washing face, gloves, handshake). Why: it limits function even without pain.
Colles Fracture (distal radius fracture) (LO6)
Pathophysiology
The end of the radius (thumb-side forearm bone) breaks near the wrist; sometimes the tip of the ulna (ulnar styloid) breaks too. Ⓜ Merck the broken end usually tips toward the back of the hand.
Etiology
Fall on an outstretched hand; most often older women (osteoporosis connection).
Signs
Puffy wrist with a "silver fork" deformity (a hump seen from the side).
Symptoms
Pain. Ⓜ Merck swelling and tenderness; numb index fingertip if the median nerve is hurt.
Diagnostics / Labs
Ⓜ Merck front and side wrist x-rays; sometimes CT. File: tenderness after a fall means check for fracture.
Risk
Older women, falls, osteoporosis. Ⓜ Merck median nerve injury, stiffness, lasting deformity, pain, later OA.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck closed reduction and splint; surgery (ORIF) if reduction fails or the joint is disrupted.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.

  • Check finger feeling, movement and thumb-to-pinky opposition. Why: swelling can injure the median nerve Ⓜ Merck.
  • Ask about bone density screening after the fracture heals. Why: wrist breaks are typical osteoporosis fractures.
Meniscus Tear and Knee Ligament Injury (including swelling of menisci) (LO4, LO6)
Pathophysiology
The menisci are two wedge-shaped cartilage cushions between femur and tibia. A twist tears a meniscus. The torn flap catches in the joint, so the knee locks, pops or gives way. Ligament tears let the bones slide, so the knee feels unstable. A lateral meniscus cyst makes a soft bump at the outer joint line.
Etiology
Hit to the inside of the knee = medial ligament strain or tear; hit to the outside = lateral ligament; sudden twisting = anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury. Meniscus tears happen with severe ligament injury.
Signs
McMurray test click = torn meniscus. Sudden locking (cannot fully straighten, often with a painful pop or click). Buckling / giving way = ligament injury. Lateral meniscus cyst = soft swelling at the middle of the front-outer joint line, bigger when the knee is half bent. Ⓜ Merck tenderness along the joint line on the side of the tear; a visible effusion.
Symptoms
Sharp, sudden pain at the inner or outer joint line; catching, locking, popping; feeling unstable. A "pop" at injury may mean a ligament tear or fracture.
Diagnostics / Labs
Ottawa knee rules (x-ray if any): pain only at patella or fibula head; age 55+; cannot flex to 90°; cannot bear weight for 4 steps. Suspected tears go to orthopedics for imaging. Ⓜ Merck MRI if symptoms do not settle after a few weeks or a serious injury is suspected.
Risk
Twisting sports, trauma. Complication: higher future risk of osteoarthritis (reason for orthopedic referral).
Expected treatment
Refer to orthopedics for imaging and possible surgery (file). Ⓜ Merck mild sprains get protection, rest, ice, compression, elevation (PRICE) and early motion; severe injuries get a splint or knee immobilizer and surgical referral; large effusions may be drained.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask exactly how the knee was hurt (inside, outside, twist, pop, can you stand, can you bend it). Why: the story points to the injured structure.
  • Apply the Ottawa knee rules. Why: they decide who needs an x-ray.
  • Compare thigh muscle size on both sides. Why: quadriceps wasting shows first on the inner side (vastus medialis).
Osgood-Schlatter Disease (LO5, LO6)
Pathophysiology
The kneecap tendon pulls hard on the bony bump below the knee (tibial tubercle) while the growth plate is still open. Repeated pulling makes tiny injuries. The bump swells and hurts. Ⓜ Merck tiny pull-off fractures of the immature bone.
Etiology
Overuse injury during the puberty growth spurt, before the growth plate closes. Ⓜ Merck ages 10 to 15; usually one knee; more common in boys, but rising in girls who play sports.
Signs
Enlarged, tender tibial tubercle; painful swelling just below the knee.
Symptoms
Pain worse with kicking, running, biking, volleyball, basketball, soccer.
Diagnostics / Labs
Exam. Ⓜ Merck side knee x-ray may show a broken-up tubercle but is not needed unless something else is suspected.
Risk
Active teens in jumping and running sports.
Expected treatment
Rest (file). Ⓜ Merck pain relievers; avoid deep knee bending; full sports stop is not needed; rarely a cast, steroid shot or surgery.
Prognosis
Self-limited; goes away with rest (file). Ⓜ Merck usually resolves in weeks to months.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask teens what they do when they get hurt. Why: teens may hide pain so they can keep playing.
  • Teach activity changes, not total sports stop. Why: it resolves with rest and complete stop is not needed Ⓜ Merck.
Hallux Valgus with Bunion, Hammertoes and Corns (LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. Hallux valgus: the big toe angles out toward the other toes; the head of the 1st metatarsal sticks out on the inner side.
  2. Bunion: the sac (bursa) over that bony point gets inflamed from pressure.
  3. The big toe loses push-off power, so weight shifts to the 2nd and 3rd metatarsal heads. This makes calluses and pain.
  4. Hammertoe: toe bends up at the MTP joint and down at the PIP joint.
  5. Corn: thick skin on top of a toe over a bony point, from shoe pressure.
Etiology
Common in RA (file). Ⓜ Merck genetics, foot rolling in (pronation), tight pointed shoes, injury; laxity at the base of the 1st metatarsal.
Signs
Big toe angled away from the midline; red bump at the inner big toe joint; calluses; hammertoes; corns; may lead to joint subluxation.
Symptoms
Pain under the 2nd and 3rd metatarsal heads; pain in shoes. Ⓜ Merck first symptom is often pain over the bump in certain shoes.
Diagnostics / Labs
Ⓜ Merck clinical exam; weight-bearing x-rays help judge hammertoe severity. Hot, red, very painful joint suggests gout or infection instead.
Risk
RA, tight shoes, genetics. Complications: corns, calluses, hammertoes, subluxation; Merck adds secondary OA.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck wide toe-box shoes, pads, orthotics; steroid shot for bursitis; surgery if pain and walking problems continue.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Look at the person's worn shoes. Why: wear patterns show abnormal gait and pressure.
  • Check calluses and skin under the toes. Why: they show abnormal friction and can break down into ulcers.
Plantar Fasciitis (LO6)
Pathophysiology
The plantar fascia is a strong band from the heel to the toes. Repeated small injuries (microtrauma) inflame and tear it where it attaches to the heel. Merck says there is usually little true inflammation, so it may better be called plantar fasciosis.
Etiology
Most frequent cause of heel pain. Repetitive microtrauma.
Signs
Localized tenderness under the heel where the fascia is torn. Ⓜ Merck pain when you press the heel with the foot bent up.
Symptoms
One-sided "throbbing, searing or piercing" pain at the inner bottom of the heel; worst in the morning or after long rest. Ⓜ Merck first steps hurt, eases in 5 to 10 minutes, returns later.
Diagnostics / Labs
Ultrasound often helps (file). Ⓜ Merck mainly history and exam; MRI if a sudden tear is suspected.
Risk
Obesity, high-arched foot, running, long standing on hard floors, recent activity change.
Expected treatment
Rest, oral pain medicine or steroids, stretching, shoe orthotics, night splints (file). Merck adds Achilles and foot stretches and proper heel height in shoes.
Prognosis
Self-limiting but may take 6 to 12 months.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask when the pain is worst. Why: first-step morning pain is the classic pattern.
  • Teach stretching and night splint use as ordered. Why: these are listed treatments and keep the fascia stretched.
Scoliosis (LO4, LO5, LO6; see AP Q#23)
Pathophysiology
The spine curves sideways in the thoracic and lumbar areas, usually with twisting of the vertebrae. The twist pushes the ribs back on one side, making a rib hump when bending forward.
Etiology
Functional: flexible; seen standing, gone when bending; may make up for another problem such as unequal leg length. Structural: fixed; seen standing and bending. Idiopathic (no known cause): shows from age 10 through the teen growth spurt; most common in teen girls.
Signs
Uneven shoulders, uneven scapulae, uneven hips (iliac crests), visible curve; rib hump on forward bend; dots marked on the spinous processes form a slight S when standing.
Symptoms
Not given in this file or Merck pages used (often found on exam).
Diagnostics / Labs
Forward bend test. Workbook order: stand behind so the whole spine shows → note shoulders, scapulae, iliac crests → ask them to bend at the waist and reach for toes → note shoulders, ribs, iliac crests while bent. ★ (see AP Q#23) Imaging: Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Risk
Teen girls, growth spurt. Most curves found by screening do not become serious.
Expected treatment
Not given in this file or Merck pages used. The file notes bracing can cause stress.
Prognosis
Idiopathic scoliosis is not always progressive.
Nursing interventions
  • Sit or stand behind the teen to view the whole spine. Why: you must see shoulders, scapulae and hips together.
  • Evaluate when a curve is found by chance or the teen or parent is worried. Why: USPSTF does not support routine screening, but concerns still need a check.
  • Be sensitive about body image. Why: the file names psychological effects of bracing as a harm.
Herniated Intervertebral Disc (Herniated Nucleus Pulposus) with Sciatica (LO4, LO6)
Pathophysiology
  1. Each disc has a soft, toothpaste-like center: the nucleus pulposus.
  2. Too much pressure tears the disc's outer ring. The center pushes out (herniates) into the spinal canal.
  3. It presses on a spinal nerve root, causing pain and inflammation.
  4. The pain runs down the leg along that nerve: radicular pain (sciatica).
Etiology
Usually after hard activity: lifting, twisting, bending while lifting, falling on the buttocks. Over 70% are at L4–L5 and L5–S1.
Signs
Positive straight leg raise (pain down the leg, worse when the foot is bent up). Pain when raising the good leg strongly suggests a herniated disc. Leaning (listing) away from the sore side. Less spine motion; low back tenderness; weaker strength and feeling in the leg. Ⓜ Merck knee or ankle reflexes may be weak or gone.
Symptoms
Low back pain plus one-sided leg pain; numbness and tingling (paresthesia) along the nerve; sitting makes it worse. Ⓜ Merck coughing and sneezing also worsen it.
Diagnostics / Labs
Straight leg raise is the most sensitive test to tell sciatica from plain back pain. Ⓜ Merck MRI or CT confirms.
Risk
Heavy lifting and twisting. Red flag Ⓜ Merck: new trouble holding or passing urine = cauda equina syndrome, an emergency.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck start with conservative care: light activity and walking (no long bed rest or traction), acetaminophen or NSAIDs, physical therapy and home exercise; sometimes steroids; surgery for severe or worsening nerve loss or cauda equina.
Prognosis
Pain usually goes away within 3 months (file). Ⓜ Merck the disc piece often dries up and is reabsorbed in 6 to 9 months.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask about bladder and bowel control. Why: cauda equina needs urgent care Ⓜ Merck.
  • Check leg strength and feeling on both sides. Why: loss of motor and sensory function shows nerve root pressure.
  • Teach safe lifting and staying lightly active. Why: lifting and twisting cause it; long bed rest is not advised Ⓜ Merck.
Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) (LO4, LO5; see Workbook SG6)
Pathophysiology
The hip socket (acetabulum) and ball (femoral head) do not form a snug fit. The ball sits behind the socket or slips in and out. Ⓜ Merck loose ligaments or position in the womb.
Etiology
Not given in this file. Merck high-risk factors: breech birth (especially girls), other position problems such as wry neck or foot deformity, family history.
Signs
Positive Ortolani: a clunk as the ball pops back in. Positive Allis: one knee lower when both knees are bent. Unequal gluteal folds after 2 to 3 months. Ⓜ Merck Barlow test (hip slides out with adduction and a push back); later, limited abduction and a shorter leg.
Symptoms
Not given in this file or Merck (infants cannot report). Untreated, the hip may later become painful Ⓜ Merck.
Diagnostics / Labs
Ortolani at every visit until age 1; Allis test. Ⓜ Merck hip ultrasound under 4 months; x-ray after 4 months.
Risk
Ⓜ Merck breech, family history, clubfoot is linked with DDH. Complications if missed: short leg, painful hip Ⓜ Merck.
Expected treatment
Refer when positive (file). Ⓜ Merck Pavlik harness holds the hips spread out and turned out; surgery if it lasts past 6 months; double diapers do not work.
Prognosis
Ⓜ Merck early treatment works best; delay makes surgery more likely.
Nursing interventions
  • Keep the baby warm and fully undressed on the back during the exam. Why: you need to see both legs; newborns lose heat fast.
  • Move gently: adduct, then lift and abduct. Why: correct technique feels for the clunk without harm.
  • Teach parents why the hip check repeats at every visit. Why: it is done until age 1.
Talipes Equinovarus (Clubfoot) (LO5)
Pathophysiology
A birth defect: the foot points down (plantar flexed) and turns in (inverted). Ⓜ Merck from an abnormal talus bone; the forefoot also turns in.
Etiology
Congenital (present at birth). Ⓜ Merck about 2 in 1000 births; both feet in up to half.
Signs
A fixed deformity: the foot reaches a right angle only with force, or not at all. A position from the womb is flexible and corrects with gentle push or scratching the outer sole.
Symptoms
Not given in this file or Merck.
Diagnostics / Labs
Flexibility check on exam.
Risk
Ⓜ Merck DDH is more common in these children; can come back as the child grows.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck repeated casts, taping or splints; surgery before 12 months if casting fails.
Prognosis
Ⓜ Merck may recur with growth.
Nursing interventions
  • Test flexible vs fixed: hold the heel and gently push the forefoot to neutral. Why: flexible positions self-correct; fixed ones need referral.
  • Also check the hips. Why: DDH is more common with clubfoot Ⓜ Merck.
Radial Head Subluxation ("Nursemaid's Elbow") (LO5, LO6)
Pathophysiology
The top of the radius (radial head) slips partly out of the ring-shaped ligament at the elbow. Ⓜ Merck in toddlers the radial head is no wider than its neck, so it slips through easily.
Etiology
Pulling or swinging a child by the hands, or yanking off clothes. Most often ages 2 to 4 Ⓜ Merck 2 to 3.
Signs
Child holds the arm against the body, straight, palm down (pronated), close to the side; will not use the arm; winces when touched; cannot turn the palm up with the elbow bent; a clunk on supination and flexion. No swelling or deformity in the case.
Symptoms
Elbow pain; cried at the moment of the pull.
Diagnostics / Labs
History plus exam. Ⓜ Merck x-rays are not needed for a simple case, but are needed if there are signs of other injury.
Risk
Toddlers lifted or swung by the arms. Nursing diagnosis in the case: risk for injury related to an unstable joint.
Expected treatment
Ⓜ Merck reduction by hyperpronation or supination-flexion; no special equipment or sedation needed.
Prognosis
Not given in this file or Merck pages used.
Nursing interventions
  • Ask exactly what happened right before the child stopped using the arm. Why: "we swung him by the arms" is the classic story.
  • Teach parents to lift under the armpits, not by the hands. Why: pulling by the hands causes it.

Workbook audit note (AP Q#21–22): the case gives bilateral swelling and pain of the DIP joints, fatigue and weight loss for a year, temperature 100.5°F, warm tender DIPs with limited ROM on both sides, and atrophy of the interosseous hand muscles. On the workbook test, answer RA. In real life, a DIP-only picture should make you think again.

D1. Bisphosphonates ★ (LO7; see AP FRAX case)

Named in the file: history question and rationale (printed page 584). Examples from Ⓜ Merck alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid (IV), ibandronate.

PartWhat to know
Drug classAntiresorptive (stops bone breakdown) bone drug.
Mechanism, plainSlows down the cells that break down bone, so the body keeps more bone.
Mechanism, technicalInhibits bone resorption, which preserves bone mass. Bone turnover drops after about 3 months of therapy Ⓜ Merck.
IndicationsFirst-line therapy for osteoporosis (file and Merck). Can cut spine and hip fractures by up to 50% Ⓜ Merck.
Adverse effects: commonIrritation of the esophagus (food tube) with oral forms Ⓜ Merck.
Adverse effects: serious (rare)Osteonecrosis of the jaw (jaw bone that will not heal) and atypical thigh bone (femur) fractures with long-term use. These fractures may follow weeks or months of thigh pain. The benefits far outweigh this small risk Ⓜ Merck.
Labs / assessmentsDXA bone density results; kidney function Ⓜ Merck safe when creatinine clearance is above 35 mL/min; swallowing problems; new thigh pain; dental health.
Nursing considerations
  • Give oral forms on an empty stomach with a full 8-oz glass of water Ⓜ Merck.
  • Patient stays upright for at least 30 minutes (60 minutes for ibandronate) and takes nothing else by mouth during that time Ⓜ Merck. Why: food blocks absorption, and lying down raises esophagus irritation.
  • Esophagus problems and upper GI symptoms are relative reasons not to use the oral form; IV is an option Ⓜ Merck.
  • Treatment length is often 5 years oral or 3 years IV, then a "drug holiday" may be considered Ⓜ Merck.
Patient teaching
  • "Take it first thing with plain water only. Then sit or stand for at least 30 minutes before you eat, drink or take other pills." Ⓜ Merck
  • "Keep up good mouth care and regular dental visits." Ⓜ Merck
  • "Tell us about any new pain in your thigh or groin." Ⓜ Merck
  • "Keep getting calcium, vitamin D and exercise. The pill works best with them." (file and Merck)

Med Math

Warning: the file gives no medicine orders. Every number below is a PRACTICE DOSE, NOT A REAL ORDER. Never use these numbers on a real patient. The daily needs for calcium and vitamin D come from the file's diet teaching. The rest are made up for math practice.

Problem 1: Calcium tablets

PRACTICE DOSE, NOT A REAL ORDER. A practice order says: calcium 1200 mg by mouth daily, given as 2 equal doses. Tablets contain 600 mg. How many tablets per dose?

  1. Dose each time = 1200 mg ÷ 2 = 600 mg.
  2. Tablets = 600 mg ÷ 600 mg per tablet = 1 tablet per dose.
  3. Check: 1 tablet × 2 doses × 600 mg = 1200 mg. ✔

Fact link: the file says women over 50 need 1200 mg calcium a day, and food calcium is absorbed better than supplements.

Problem 2: Vitamin D liquid

PRACTICE DOSE, NOT A REAL ORDER. Practice order: vitamin D 800 IU by mouth daily. The practice liquid has 400 IU per 1 mL. How many mL?

  1. Formula: (Want ÷ Have) × Volume.
  2. (800 IU ÷ 400 IU) × 1 mL = 2 × 1 mL = 2 mL.

Fact link: the file lists 800 IU a day after age 70.

Problem 3: FRAX conversions for C.M. ★ (see AP FRAX case)

C.M. is 5 feet 7 inches tall and weighs 124 lb. The FRAX tool needs kg and cm.

  1. Weight: 124 lb ÷ 2.2 = 56.4 kg (rounded).
  2. Height: 5 ft × 12 = 60 in. 60 + 7 = 67 in. 67 × 2.54 = 170.2 cm.
  3. BMI (body mass index) = kg ÷ (meters × meters). 170.2 cm = 1.702 m. 1.702 × 1.702 = 2.897. 56.4 ÷ 2.897 = about 19.5.

Note: low body weight is a risk factor for osteoporosis.

Problem 4: The housekeeper's acetaminophen (case history math)

This is what a case patient took on her own. It is NOT an order and NOT a dose to copy. In the textbook's Case Study 2, L.M. took 6 extra-strength acetaminophen tablets (500 mg each) a day, in 3 divided doses.

  1. Total per day = 6 × 500 mg = 3000 mg.
  2. Per dose = 3000 mg ÷ 3 = 1000 mg.
  3. Tablets per dose = 1000 ÷ 500 = 2 tablets.

Nursing thought: always total up a patient's daily over-the-counter medicine. The file says to ask how many OTC medicines a person takes daily.

Problem 5: Weight-based pediatric practice

PRACTICE DOSE, NOT A REAL ORDER. "Drug Z" is made up. A 2-year-old weighs 12 kg. Practice order: Drug Z 10 mg/kg per dose. Supply: 100 mg per 5 mL.

  1. Dose = 10 mg × 12 kg = 120 mg.
  2. Concentration = 100 mg ÷ 5 mL = 20 mg per mL.
  3. Volume = 120 mg ÷ 20 mg/mL = 6 mL.
  4. Check: 6 mL × 20 mg/mL = 120 mg. ✔

Unfolding Case Study

Practice case built from facts in the file and Merck. The patient is made up. Merck facts are marked Ⓜ Merck.

Meet Ms. Rosa Delgado, 79

Retired teacher. Lives alone. Drinks 2 glasses of wine a day. Never had a bone density scan.

Stage 1: Arrival in the ED (0 hours)

Story: Rosa tripped on a throw rug at home. She fell on her outstretched right hand. Her wrist is puffy. From the side it has a hump, like the back of a dinner fork.

What the nurse notices:

  • Fall on outstretched hand + older woman + "silver fork" hump = suspect a Colles fracture. A Colles fracture is a break of the end of the radius (forearm bone on the thumb side) (see Table 23-4).
  • Tenderness after a fall means check for fracture.
  • Fracture pain is sharp and gets worse with movement.

What the nurse does:

  • Compares the right wrist to the left wrist.
  • Supports the arm. Uses gentle movement. Does not force range of motion.
  • Reports the findings so the provider can order an x-ray.
  • Asks history questions: past falls, past fractures, smoking, alcohol, calcium and vitamin D, medicines, mobility aids, and whether she ever had a DXA scan (a low-dose x-ray that measures bone density).

Stage 2: Change in condition (6 hours after splint)

Story: The wrist was splinted and wrapped. Now Rosa says the pain is "way worse than before." The pain jumps when the nurse gently straightens her fingers. The forearm feels tight.

What the nurse notices:

  • Pain out of proportion to the injury, worse with passive stretch of the muscles, and a tense compartment = possible compartment syndrome Ⓜ Merck. This means pressure builds inside a closed muscle space and blood flow to the tissue drops Ⓜ Merck.
  • Tight bandages and splints can be a cause Ⓜ Merck. The forearm is one of the most common sites Ⓜ Merck.
  • A pulse can still be present. Tissue blood flow can stop long before the pulse is lost Ⓜ Merck. Pain comes first; then paresthesia (tingling), paralysis, pallor and pulselessness Ⓜ Merck.

What the nurse does:

  • Calls the provider right away. This is urgent. Diagnosis must be made before pallor or pulselessness show up Ⓜ Merck.
  • Uses SBAR (see Section Q).
  • Expect the provider's first steps: remove the tight wrap or splint, give pain relief, and check compartment pressure. Surgery called fasciotomy is often needed Ⓜ Merck.

Stage 3: Intervention and bone health check (day 2)

Story: The pressure problem was treated. Rosa is stable. Now the team looks at why a simple trip broke her wrist.

What the nurse notices:

  • Rosa has an upper-back hump (kyphosis) and her head tilts back. She says she is "shorter than I used to be."
  • Height loss of 3 to 5 cm with aging comes from thinner discs. Bigger losses in the 70s and 80s come from osteoporotic collapse of vertebrae.
  • Her Get Up and Go Test takes 15 seconds. That is 12 seconds or more = increased fall risk.
  • Risk factors: older woman, fracture after a minor fall, alcohol, no bone scan.

What the nurse does:

  • Starts fall precautions (see Section AA).
  • Tells the provider she has never had a DXA scan. The file says all women should have one by age 65.
  • Mentions the FRAX tool. FRAX gives her 10-year chance of a hip fracture and a major bone fracture.
  • Notes that the file lists bisphosphonates as first-line therapy for osteoporosis. That choice belongs to the provider.

Stage 4: Outcome (discharge, day 3)

What the nurse does: Teaches with the teach-back method (see Section Y):

  • Calcium: women over 50 need 1200 mg a day. Food calcium is absorbed better than pills.
  • Vitamin D: 800 IU a day after age 70.
  • Alcohol: women, no more than 1 drink a day.
  • Exercise: strength and balance work. Start easy. Work up to 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week.
  • Home: remove throw rugs, add lights, add grab bars, safe stairs. Check vision and hearing every year. Review medicines that affect balance.

Good outcome: Rosa explains back the plan in her own words. She names the throw rug as her first fix. She has a follow-up for a DXA scan.

Merck pages: Compartment Syndrome; Osteoporosis.

"What Went Wrong?" Error Spotting

Answers are blurred. Decide first, then tap to check.

Read the story. Find the hidden error. Then check the answer.

Scenario 1: The car crash neck

A student nurse sees a man after a car crash. He says his neck hurts. To be thorough, the student asks him to touch his chin to his chest and turn his head side to side to measure ROM.

Error: Neck ROM was tested when neck trauma was suspected.

Correct action: Do not test cervical ROM if neck trauma is suspected. Keep the neck still and report to the provider.

Scenario 2: The rough RA wrist

A patient has a hot, swollen right wrist from an RA flare. The nurse grabs the wrist, pushes it quickly past the point of pain to "get the full number," and charts "wrist flexion 45°." The left wrist is not checked.

Errors:

  • Rough handling of an inflamed joint. This can cause pain and muscle spasm.
  • Forcing motion past pain.
  • No comparison to the opposite side.

Correct action: Support the joint. Use firm support, gentle motion, and a gentle return to rest. Do active ROM first. Try passive ROM only gently, with muscles relaxed. Always compare to the matching joint on the other side. Normal active and passive ROM should be the same. ★ (see Workbook SG4)

Scenario 3: The "normal" clunk

During a 2-month-old's checkup, the nurse does the Ortolani check. A clunk is felt as the knees are moved apart. The nurse thinks it's a normal joint "crack" and charts "hips normal."

Error: A clunk during Ortolani is not normal. It means the femoral head was out and popped back in.

Correct action: Chart a positive Ortolani sign and refer. Normal is smooth with no sound. ★ (see Workbook SG6)

Bonus Scenario 4: The leg length guess

A nurse measures leg length from the belly button to each ankle and says, "One leg is truly shorter."

Error: Belly button to ankle measures apparent leg length, not true leg length.

Correct action: True leg length = from the anterior superior iliac spine (front hip bone point) to the medial malleolus (inner ankle bone), crossing the inner knee. Within 1 cm = equal. ★ (see Workbook SG5)

Nursing Diagnoses

NANDA-style format: Problem related to (R/T) cause as evidenced by (AEB) findings. ★ marks labels the textbook itself uses.

    Goal: Patient will button a shirt and open a jar without help within 1 week.
  2. Acute pain ★ R/T joint inflammation (RA flare) AEB red, swollen, warm, tender MCP and PIP joints and a pain report.
    Goal: Patient will report pain at a level they find acceptable within 24 hours.
  3. Chronic pain R/T degenerative joint changes (OA) AEB knee pain for 2 years that worsens through the day.
    Goal: Patient will name 2 ways to manage pain that let them keep working, by the next visit.
  4. Risk for falls R/T kyphosis, muscle weakness, and alcohol use. (A risk diagnosis has no "AEB" because the problem has not happened yet.)
    Goal: Patient will have no falls during the hospital stay.
  5. Risk for injury ★ R/T unstable joint (radial head subluxation) in a toddler.
    Goal: Parents will state why not to swing or lift the child by the hands before discharge.
  6. Self-care deficit (dressing/grooming) ★ R/T hand deformity (ulnar deviation, swan-neck) AEB inability to fasten buttons or brush hair.
    Goal: Patient will dress with the help of adaptive tools within 2 weeks.
  7. Deficient knowledge (bone health) R/T no past teaching AEB patient says "I didn't know my wine affects my bones" and has never had a DXA scan.
    Goal: Patient will state daily calcium and vitamin D needs and the alcohol limit before discharge.

Say: "Falls can break bones, so let's make your home safer. Add more lights. Make your stairs safe. Take up throw rugs. Put in grab bars where you need them. Get your eyes and ears checked every year. Ask your provider to look at your medicines, because some can make you dizzy. Keep exercising to build strength."

Teach-back: "What are three things you'll change at home this week?"

3. Joint protection and RA self-care

Say: "During a flare, your joints are inflamed, so plan rest periods during your day. Do your prescribed exercises every day, even when you feel okay. Take your aspirin the way your provider told you. Don't lower the dose on your own. Aspirin and similar pain medicines can upset or bleed in your stomach. Call if you have stomach pain or black stools."

Teach-back: "How will you take your aspirin, and what stomach signs would make you call us?"

Gap note: The file does not list joint protection devices or splints for RA. For carpal tunnel, Merck lists splinting and work-station changes (ergonomics) Ⓜ Merck.

4. Back safety

Say: "A disc in your back is like a jelly donut. Hard lifting, twisting, or lifting while bent over can squeeze the jelly out. Then it presses on a nerve. If your job has heavy lifting or the same motion over and over, let's look at ways to make it safer. Avoid lifting and twisting at the same time."

Teach-back: "Show me how you'd pick up a laundry basket without twisting."

Warning sign to teach Ⓜ Merck: new trouble holding or passing urine with back pain is an emergency Ⓜ Merck.

5. FRAX case teaching plan for C.M. ★ (see AP FRAX case)

Who: C.M., 81-year-old White woman. 5 ft 7 in, 124 lb. No past fractures. No smoking or steroids. Drinks 1 to 2 alcohol units a day. Has osteoporosis, femoral neck T-score −2.0.

What FRAX gives: her 10-year risk (as a percent) of a hip fracture and of a major osteoporotic fracture.

Risk management (plain words):

  • "Your bones are thinner than they should be, and your weight is on the low side. Both raise your risk of breaking a bone."
  • "Cut alcohol to no more than 1 drink a day. Drinking thins bones and makes falls more likely."
  • "Aim for 1200 mg of calcium and 800 units of vitamin D a day, mostly from food."
  • "Do strength and balance exercise most days."
  • "Let's make your home fall-proof: lights, rugs, grab bars, safe stairs."

Follow-up care: Talk with the provider about treatment (the file names bisphosphonates as first-line). Yearly vision and hearing checks. Ask about a vitamin D blood test. Repeat FRAX when full data is available.

Teach-back: "What is the one change you'll make first, and who will you see next?"

6. Scoliosis screening for teens

Say (to a worried teen and parent): "Scoliosis is a sideways curve of the spine. It shows up most during the growth spurt before and during the teen years. Most small curves found at school checks don't get worse, so routine checks aren't recommended for teens without symptoms. But if you're worried, we can check now. You'll stand with feet apart and slowly bend forward to touch your toes. I'll look for one side of your ribs sitting higher, and check if your shoulders and hips are level."

Teach-back: "What will I be looking for when you bend forward?"

For teens in sports: wear the right protective gear. Report injuries. Teens may hide pain so they can keep playing.

Safety and Infection Control

Infection control

  • Hand hygiene before and after the exam. The lab write-up starts with "wash hands."
  • Standard precautions (treat all body fluids as possibly infectious) for every patient. General nursing rule, not spelled out in this file.
  • Gloves if skin is broken or draining. Examples from this chapter: an infected ingrown toenail, a prepatellar bursitis where infection can spread into nearby tissue, and foot ulcers. Glove use is a general rule; the conditions are from the file.
  • Plantar warts are caused by HPV (human papillomavirus). Use gloves when touching them. (Glove use is a general rule.)
  • Contact or droplet precautions: Not given in this file or Merck for these musculoskeletal conditions.

Fall precautions

  • Ask about falls. A past fall raises the risk of future falls.
  • Use the Get Up and Go Test. 12 seconds or more = increased fall risk.
  • Ask about mobility aids (cane, walker).
  • Teach home safety: lighting, safe stairs, remove throw rugs, grab bars.
  • Alcohol raises fall risk.

Safety during the exam

  • Keep the person safe during every movement.
  • No neck ROM if neck trauma is suspected.
  • Support each joint at rest. Be gentle with inflamed joints.
  • After an injury, use the Ottawa rules to decide on x-rays. Knee: pain at the kneecap or top of the fibula alone, age 55 or older, cannot bend the knee to 90°, or cannot take 4 steps. Ankle/midfoot: pain near the ankle bones or midfoot plus bone tenderness or inability to take 4 steps.
  • Full elbow extension after a fall usually rules out a fracture.
  • Newborns: use a warming table to keep the baby warm while undressed.
  • Children: teach parents not to lift or swing a child by the hands (nursemaid's elbow risk).
  • Sports: helmets, mouth guards and pads. Adult supervision lowers injury risk.

Casts, splints and wraps Ⓜ Merck

  • Watch for pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, and a tight feeling. These suggest compartment syndrome Ⓜ Merck.
  • Do not wait for a lost pulse. Report early Ⓜ Merck.

Bleeding

  • Patients on daily aspirin or NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory pain medicines) need screening for stomach pain and bleeding.

Seizures

  • Not applicable to this chapter. (Epilepsy is only named as a condition linked to Dupuytren contracture.)

Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Cultural factors (from the file's Genetics and Environment box)

  • Bone mineral density (BMD) differs a lot by race and ethnicity. Low BMD strongly predicts hip and spine fractures.
  • Non-Hispanic Black adults have higher BMD and lose bone more slowly than other groups.
  • But Black women and men have higher death rates and longer hospital stays after a hip fracture. Lower risk does not mean lower stakes.
  • White women in the US are more likely to get osteoporosis than Black, Hispanic and Asian women.
  • Asian and White women have similar BMD, but Asian women have lower osteoporosis rates.
  • Hispanic BMD varies by country of origin. More research is needed.
  • Main risks for all groups: older age, female sex, past fracture. So teach prevention to all women, whatever their race or ethnicity.
  • FRAX uses country- and group-specific versions (the workbook picked "US Caucasian").
  • Sex differences: RA is about 2.5 times more common in women. Ankylosing spondylitis is more common in males. Dupuytren is mostly in men over 40.
  • Diet teaching: "eat the rainbow" works across cultures. Ask about usual foods and dairy, and suggest calcium sources that fit them (like soy milk or fortified juice).
  • Single palmar crease can occur with some genetic conditions such as Down syndrome. Describe findings without judgment.

Ethical and legal points

  • Privacy: Drape so you can see the body part fully without exposing the person needlessly. The spine exam uses a gown open at the back.
  • Consent: Explain each move before you do it, and stop if the person says stop. General nursing ethics; not spelled out in this file.
  • Dignity in chronic illness: Ask how the illness affects family, friends, self-image and roles. Watch for low self-esteem, loss of independence, body image changes and social isolation.
  • Teens: They may hide pain so they can keep playing sports. Ask in a safe, private way.
  • Scoliosis screening: Routine screening of teens without symptoms is not supported because harms (extra imaging, visits, stress from bracing) can outweigh benefits.
  • Elder abuse Ⓜ Merck: A fracture can be blamed on osteoporosis when it was really abuse Ⓜ Merck. Red flags: a delay in getting care, stories that don't match, an injury too severe for the explanation, or a caregiver who won't leave the patient alone Ⓜ Merck. Musculoskeletal signs include a previously undiagnosed fracture, unexplained pain and an unexplained gait problem Ⓜ Merck.
  • If abuse is suspected, interview the older adult alone for part of the visit Ⓜ Merck. Document injuries in detail, in the patient's own words when possible Ⓜ Merck. Reporting to Adult Protective Services is required in most US states Ⓜ Merck.
  • Child injuries: The nursemaid's elbow case came from swinging a child, an accident. Teach parents kindly. Child abuse fracture patterns: Not given in this file or Merck (not researched here).

Head-to-Toe Assessment Checklist

Before you start

  • Equipment: tape measure, skin marking pen, and sometimes a goniometer (a tool that measures joint angles).
  • Order: head to toe, proximal to distal (center of the body outward), midline outward.
  • Comfort: drape the person so you see the body part without exposing more than needed. Support each joint at rest. Muscles must be relaxed.
  • Inflamed joint: firm support, gentle movement, gentle return to rest. Rough handling causes pain and muscle spasm.
  • Compare both sides. Expect them to match.
  • Same 4 steps at every joint: inspect (look), palpate (feel), ROM (move), muscle strength (push against you).
  • Watch daily tasks too: gait (walking pattern), posture, sitting down, standing up, taking off a jacket, holding a pen, getting up from lying down.

Body System "Home Base"

The whole system on one page

  • What it is: bones, joints, and muscles working as one team.
  • Bones: 206 of them. Over half are in the hands and feet. Bone is hard and dense, but it keeps rebuilding itself all life long. This rebuilding is called remodeling.
  • Joints: the "working parts." They make daily tasks possible, like dressing and walking. These tasks are called activities of daily living (ADLs).
  • Muscles: 40% to 50% of body weight. This chapter covers skeletal muscle, the kind you control on purpose.
  • Glue and padding: ligaments, tendons, cartilage, bursae, and the discs between the vertebrae.

Topic drill 1: Joints – structure and movement

★ SG1. "Differentiate synovial from nonsynovial joints."

— No answer given in key — answer from textbook p. 573:

  • Nonsynovial joints have no joint space with fluid. There are two kinds:
  • Fibrous joints are joined by fibrous tissue. They do not move. Example: the sutures (seams) of the skull.
  • Cartilaginous joints have discs of fibrocartilage (tough, rubbery tissue) between the bones. They move only a little. Example: the vertebrae (back bones).

★ SG4. "Differentiate testing of active range of motion versus passive range of motion."

— No answer given in key — answer from textbook p. 586:

  • Range of motion (ROM) = how far a joint can move.
  • Active ROM: the person moves the joint by themselves (on purpose). You can show the move on your own body first. You may hold the body part just above the joint steady.
  • Passive ROM: done if you see a limit. The person relaxes. You hold the joint steady with one hand and slowly move it with your other hand to its limit.
  • Normal: active and passive ranges are the same.
  • Limited ROM is the most sensitive sign of joint disease. Disease inside the joint (like arthritis) limits all directions, in both active and passive motion. Injury outside the joint (one tendon, ligament or nerve) limits only some directions, mostly active motion.
  • Merck says: a limit in active motion can come from weakness, pain, stiffness or a mechanical block. A limit in passive motion points to a mechanical cause, such as scarring, swelling or deformity. Ⓜ Merck

★ SG5. "Explain the method for measuring leg length."

Audio Summary Script
Uses your device voice. Works offline.

About 4–5 minutes aloud. Good for text-to-speech.

Hey Josh. Today we are walking through the musculoskeletal system. That's your bones, your joints, and your muscles. Let's go.

Picture a building. The skeleton is the frame. It has 206 bones, like posts and beams. Those bones do five jobs. They hold you up. They let you move. They protect soft things, like your brain and heart. They make blood cells in the marrow. And they store calcium and phosphorus, like a mineral bank.

Now, the real stars are the joints. A joint is where two bones meet. Some joints never move, like the seams in your skull. Some move a little, like your vertebrae. And some move freely. Those are synovial joints. Inside, there's a slippery fluid, like grease on gears. The bone ends wear a smooth cap of cartilage. Here's the twist. Cartilage has no blood supply. It eats by soaking up fluid when you move. So movement literally feeds your joints.

Ligaments tie bone to bone. Tendons tie muscle to bone. And little fluid sacs called bursae sit where things rub. Remember SITS for the rotator cuff. Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis.

So how do you examine all this? Go head to toe. Go from the center outward. And always compare left to right. For every joint, you do four things. Look. Feel. Move. Test strength.

When you look, swelling matters. It means the joint is irritated. When you feel, warmth and tenderness mean inflammation. A boggy, doughy feeling means the joint lining is thick. When you move, start with active range of motion. That's the person moving on their own. If something stops short, you gently move it yourself. That's passive. Normally, both match. And here's a big one. A limited range of motion is the most sensitive sign of joint disease.

Now, three patients from the chapter.

First, M.T. She's 45. She has rheumatoid arthritis. Her hands are swollen and burning. Her wrists and knuckles are red and tender. Her fingers drift toward the pinky side. That's ulnar deviation. She's tired, and she lost weight. That's the RA story. It hits both sides. It brings whole-body symptoms. It's worst in the morning. And moving actually helps.

Second, L.M. She's 58. She cleans hotel rooms. Her knees hurt for two years. No morning stiffness. But the pain builds all day long. That's the osteoarthritis pattern. Wear and tear. Then her knee gave out on the stairs. Think Ottawa knee rules. Can she bend to ninety degrees? Can she take four steps? Is she over fifty-five? Those answers decide the x-ray.

Third, A.S. He's two. His parents swung him by the arms. Now he won't use his right arm. He holds it straight against his body. When the forearm turns palm up and bends, there's a clunk. That's nursemaid's elbow. The head of the radius slipped out of place.

Pocket tests. For the wrist, the Phalen test. Backs of hands together, wrists bent, one full minute. Numbness means carpal tunnel. Tap the median nerve. Tingling is a positive Tinel sign. For the knee, a tiny bit of fluid shows with the bulge sign. More fluid shows with ballottement, where the kneecap taps the bone. A click when you straighten a twisted knee is McMurray. That's a torn meniscus. For the back, raise the straight leg. If shooting leg pain returns, think herniated disc.

Babies get their own exam. Check every hip with the Ortolani maneuver until age one. A clunk means the hip popped back in. That's a referral. Look at knee height too. One low knee is a positive Allis sign. And feel the collarbone. It's the bone most often broken at birth.

Teens? Watch the spine. Stand behind them. Have them bend and reach for their toes. A rib hump on one side points to scoliosis.

Pregnancy brings a bigger low-back curve, a forward neck, and later a waddle. Older adults often get kyphosis, a slight bend at the hips and knees, and shorter height from thinner discs. But their long bones don't shrink. And boggy knuckles are never normal aging.

Bones need protection. Calcium. Vitamin D. Strength and balance exercise. No smoking. Limit alcohol. Women get a DEXA bone scan by age sixty-five. And FRAX can estimate fracture risk for the next ten years.

That's the chapter. Look, feel, move, test. Compare both sides. And always ask how the joint lets the person live their life. You've got this.

Quick word bank for this part

These words show up a lot below. Here is what they mean in plain words.

  • Synovial joint = a joint that moves freely. A sac of slippery fluid sits between the bones.
  • Synovial fluid = the slippery fluid inside that sac. The book compares it to grease on gears.
  • ROM (range of motion) = how far a joint can move.
  • Active ROM = the person moves the joint. Passive ROM = you move it for them while they relax.
  • Effusion = extra fluid inside a joint.
  • Palpate = to feel with your hands.
  • Bursa = a small fluid sac that stops rubbing between parts.
  • Tendon = a cord that ties muscle to bone. Ligament = a band that ties bone to bone.
  • Crepitation (crepitus) = a crunchy, grating sound or feel when a joint moves.
  • Proximal = closer to the trunk. Distal = farther from the trunk.
  • DIP, PIP, MCP = finger knuckles. DIP is the one nearest the fingertip. PIP is the middle one. MCP is the big knuckle where the finger meets the hand.
  • Bilateral = both sides. Symmetric = the same on both sides.

1. Joint types: "Joint City" (story)

A joint is where two or more bones meet. Picture a city with three neighborhoods.

  • Fibrous Street (frozen statues): The bones are glued with tough tissue. Nobody moves. Immovable. Example: the sutures of the skull (the seams between skull bones).
  • Cartilage Court (bouncy neighbors): Bones sit on little cushions called fibrocartilage discs. They can wiggle a little. Slightly movable. Example: the vertebrae (back bones).
  • Synovial Dance Hall (free dancers): The bones sit in a closed room called the joint cavity. The walls (the synovial membrane) spray oil called synovial fluid, like grease on gears. The dancers glide. Freely movable.

Dance hall extras: cartilage is the smooth floor on the bone ends. It has no blood supply and "eats" by sipping synovial fluid when the joint moves. That is why moving keeps cartilage fed. Ligaments are the security guards who tie bone to bone. Bursae are little water pillows where things rub. ★ (see Workbook SG1) (see Fig 23-1: Fig 23-1)

3. Muscle grading 0 to 5: "The Gravity Gym" (character)

Meet six gym members. Each one is one grade (see the grading table on printed p. 587).

  • 0 = Zero Zed: No contraction at all. 0% of normal.
  • 1 = Twitchy Trace: Only a slight flicker of the muscle. No real movement. 10%.
  • 2 = Poor Pat: Moves the full range only when gravity is taken away. 25%.
  • 3 = Fair Fran: Beats gravity, but you can't push on Fran at all. 50%.
  • 4 = Good Gabe: Beats gravity and some push from you. 75%.
  • 5 = Normal Nina: Beats gravity and your full push. 100%.

Order of the rating names (0 to 5): Zero, Trace, Poor, Fair, Good, Normal. Sentence hook: "Zebras Trot Past Fat Goats Nicely." Always compare right and left. Strength should be equal on both sides.

Merck uses the same 0 to 5 idea (the MRC scale) Ⓜ Merck.

4. RA vs OA: "The Royal Attacker vs the Old Road" (story)

RA = Rheumatoid arthritis, a body-wide autoimmune disease. Autoimmune means the immune system attacks the body's own tissue. OA = Osteoarthritis, a wear-and-tear disease that is noninflammatory.

  • The Royal Attacker (RA): An army marches in matching rows. It attacks the same joints on both sides (symmetric, bilateral). It strikes in the morning. Joints wake up stiff after rest. Moving actually helps. The soldiers make the knuckles boggy (soft and spongy), warm and tender at the MCP joints (the big knuckles). The whole kingdom feels sick: fatigue, weight loss, low fever. ★ (see AP Q#21, Q#22)
  • The Old Road (OA): A road worn down by years of traffic. Damage is uneven (asymmetric). It hurts later in the day after use. Moving makes it worse. It leaves hard, bony, nontender bumps. No boggy MCPs.

Morning stiffness timer Ⓜ Merck: RA lasts more than 60 minutes; OA lasts under 30 minutes Ⓜ Merck. Merck also says RA spares the DIP joints (the joints nearest the fingertips) Ⓜ Merck.

Audit flag for Q#21/22: The workbook case puts the findings at the DIP joints, but the key answer is RA. The textbook and Merck say DIP nodes point to OA and RA usually spares the DIP. Learn the key answer for the quiz. Know the textbook rule for real life.

(see Table 23-1: RA joint, OA joint)

5. Heberden vs Bouchard nodes: "Alphabet Elevator" (acronym)

Both are hard, nontender, bony nodes of OA. Ride an elevator from the palm up to the fingertip.

  • Alphabet order: B comes before H.
  • Finger order from the palm: PIP comes before DIP.
  • So: B = Bouchard = PIP (middle knuckle). H = Heberden = DIP (top knuckle).
  • Extra hook: Heberden is at the Head of the finger.

Bouchard nodes are less common than Heberden nodes. ★ (see Workbook SG9) (see Table 23-4: Bouchard and Heberden photo)

Bonus RA finger shapes ★ (see Workbook SG8): Swan-neck = middle knuckle (PIP) bends backward and the tip (DIP) bends down, like a swan's curved neck. Boutonniere = the knuckle looks pushed through a buttonhole ("boutonniere" is French for buttonhole) (see Table 23-4 swan-neck and boutonniere).

6. Ortolani: "Oh, it's back IN!" (sound effect)

DDH = developmental dysplasia of the hip. It means the baby's thighbone ball does not sit well in the hip socket.

Act it out with a stuffed animal:

  1. Baby on back, knees bent. Your thumbs on the inner thighs. Fingers on the outer hips.
  2. Bring the knees together (adduct) until your thumbs touch.
  3. Gently lift and spread the knees apart and down (abduct).
  4. Normal = smooth and silent. A clunk = the ball pops back IN = positive Ortolani. Refer.

Hook: "Ortolani = 'Oh, it's back in!' The hip goes out-to-in." Do it at every visit until age 1. ★ (see Workbook SG6) (see Fig 23-46)

7. Barlow: "Barlow Bounces it out" (rhyme)

Barlow is the partner test Ⓜ Merck. The hip slides OUT when you bring the leg in (adduct) and push backward Ⓜ Merck.

Rhyme: "Barlow bounces it out the back. Ortolani brings it back on track."

Note: the Jarvis text in this file teaches Ortolani. Barlow comes from Merck.

8. Phalen and Tinel: "Reverse Prayer and the Woodpecker" (visual scene)

Carpal tunnel syndrome = the median nerve gets squeezed in a tunnel at the wrist.

  • Phalen = Reverse Prayer: Push the backs of both hands together, fingers pointing down. The wrists bend to 90°. Hold 60 seconds. Positive = numbness and burning. Hook: "Phalen = Flexed and Frozen for a minute." (see Fig 23-28)
  • Tinel = Woodpecker: A woodpecker taps on the median nerve at the wrist. Positive = burning and tingling along the nerve. Hook: "Tinel = Tap = Tingle." (see Fig 23-29)

The squeezed nerve feeds the thumb, index and middle finger. Late sign: the thumb muscle pad (thenar eminence) shrinks (see Table 23-4 thenar atrophy). People often wake at night and shake the hand Ⓜ Merck.

9. Bulge sign: "Milk the Knee, Watch the Wave" (hands-on)

Try it with a water balloon in a sock.

  1. Stroke up the inner (medial) side of the knee 2 to 3 times. This pushes fluid away.
  2. Tap the outer (lateral) side.
  3. Watch the inner hollow. A little wave (bulge) comes back = fluid in the knee.

The bulge sign finds very small fluid amounts, 4 to 8 mL. For bigger amounts use ballottement: push the kneecap down sharply. It bobs through fluid like an ice cube and taps the bone. (see Fig 23-32, Fig 23-33)

10. McMurray: "The Meniscus Click Song" (song, to the tune of "Twinkle Twinkle")

The menisci are two wedge-shaped cartilage cushions inside the knee.

"Hold the heel and bend the knee,

Turn it out and press it in (valgus) for me,

Slowly straighten, listen well,

A click means a torn meniscus to tell."

Use it when there was trauma followed by locking, giving way, or knee pain. A click = positive = torn meniscus. Refer to orthopedics. (see Fig 23-36)

11. Thomas test: "Thomas the Tank Engine Stays on the Track" (character)

The person lies on their back. They pull one knee to the chest. The other leg is the train. It must stay flat on the track (the table).

If the other thigh lifts off the table, the train jumped the track. That is a positive Thomas test. It shows a flexion deformity of the opposite hip (the hip is stuck partly bent). Normal hip flexion with knee bent is about 120°. (see Fig 23-30)

12. Scoliosis screen: "Be Level, Bend, Level" chant (rhythm)

Scoliosis = a sideways curve of the spine, usually with some twist. Clap on each step:

  1. "Behind!" Stand behind the person so you see the full spine. (Step 3)
  2. "Level!" Check shoulders, scapulae (shoulder blades), iliac crests (hip bones). (Step 2)
  3. "Bend!" Person bends at the waist and reaches for the toes. (Step 5)
  4. "Level again!" Check shoulders, ribs, iliac crests while bent. (Step 7)

Correct order = 3, 2, 5, 7. Steps 1, 4, 6 are not used. ★ (see AP Q#23) Look for a rib hump on one side when bending. (see Table 23-7 scoliosis)

13. Kyphosis vs lordosis: "Turtle Shell vs Sway Dancer" (visual pair)

  • Kyphosis = too much outward curve of the upper (thoracic) back, a "hunchback." Picture a turtle shell. Common in older adults. The head tilts back to make up for it, like a "figure 3" from the side. Also common in teens with poor posture. ★ (see AP Q#19) (see Fig 23-54)
  • Lordosis = too much inward curve of the low (lumbar) back, a "sway back." Picture a dancer leaning back with belly forward. Common in pregnancy, toddlers, and people with obesity. ★ (see AP Q#20) (see Fig 23-53)

Hook: "Kyphosis = Knobby top. Lordosis = Low and Leaning."

14. Valgus vs varus: "aiR and gLue" (letter trick)

The book's own trick: link the r and the l.

  • Genu vaRum = knees apaRt = bowlegs. Air between the knees. Normal for about 1 year after a child starts walking.
  • Genu vaLgum = knees together, like gLue = knock-knees. Normal from about age 2 to 3½.

Both count when the gap is more than 2.5 cm. (see Fig 23-51)

15. Gout big toe: "The Crystal Party Crasher" (story)

Uric acid builds up in the blood. It forms sharp urate crystals, like broken glass, in the joint. The crystals crash a party in the big toe joint. The toe turns red, swollen, warm, with extreme, throbbing pain.

Who invites the crasher? Surgery, trauma, diuretics (water pills), alcohol. The party is more common with obesity, metabolic syndrome, high blood pressure, high blood fats.

If the crasher keeps coming back for years, it leaves hard white lumps called tophi (chronic gout). Gout in the big toe is called podagra Ⓜ Merck. First attacks usually involve one joint and fade in about 7 to 10 days Ⓜ Merck. (see Table 23-6 acute gout)

16. Osteoporosis risk: "FRAGILE" (acronym)

Osteoporosis = bone loses mass and gets porous (full of holes). Fracture risk goes up, especially wrist, hip, and spine.

  • F = Female and past Fracture (also a parent with a hip fracture, from the FRAX tool)
  • R = Rheumatoid arthritis (a FRAX question)
  • A = Age (older) and Alcohol (FRAX asks about 3 or more units a day)
  • G = Glucocorticoids (steroid medicines, a FRAX question; Merck says chronic use) Ⓜ Merck
  • I = Inactivity (sedentary life speeds bone aging)
  • L = Low weight/small body size, Low estrogen, Low BMD (bone mineral density)
  • E = Early menopause, plus Ethnicity (White women in the US are more likely to get osteoporosis than Black, Hispanic and Asian women). Also smoking: cigarettes increase bone loss.

Color Theory

One simple code of 12 colors. Animations and drawings in other sections can reuse it. Text on the page stays black, so readability stays high. Each swatch is a color block only.

How to use the code

  • Draw a synovial joint: bone ivory, cartilage blue cap, gold fluid, gray ligament straps, rose tendon to red muscle.
  • Animate RA: the gold synovium swells and glows orange (inflammation), then the blue cartilage gets "bitten" away.
  • Animate OA: the blue cartilage thins and cracks. Ivory bone spurs grow at the edges. No orange glow (OA is noninflammatory).
  • Carpal tunnel: a pink nerve gets squeezed in a tunnel. Pink sparks fly to the thumb, index and middle finger.
  • Any finding card: green border = normal, purple border = abnormal.
  • Lifespan pages: aqua header for kids, sepia header for older adults.
